The year, in the only order that works

Last session you sorted Northgate's 30 services into 10 projects. Tonight you put them on a calendar - and discover that most of the sequencing was never yours to decide. Lake before warehouse before BI before ML before agents: the dependency ladder sets the spine of year one, and your actual leadership decisions live in the gaps it leaves. You will build the quarter-by-quarter roadmap, learn what genuinely runs in parallel and what only pretends to, and then do the most instructive thing possible: break a dependency on purpose and watch the blast radius. You leave treating the roadmap as what it really is - a versioned contract between you and everyone you have made promises to.

Part 1 · covers sequencing by dependency, the ladder

The ladder decides first 8 min live

Before any prioritization framework gets a vote, physics votes: lake before warehouse before BI before ML before agents. Dashboards read from a governed warehouse; models train on the data those pipelines produce; agents act on what the models and the governance layer make safe. This is the "hierarchy of needs" logic for data offices - each layer of ambition stands on the one below, and skipping a rung does not speed you up, it moves the delay downstream and adds rework interest. The leader's job is not to invent the sequence. It is to defend it in rooms where it is inconvenient.

LiveDefending the ladder in the boardroom5 min▶

Nobody argues with the ladder in the abstract. They argue with it one exception at a time. The three you will hear in year one:

  • "Just one dashboard, straight off the source systems." Feels harmless, becomes the permanent shadow pipeline nobody governs. Answer: yes to the dashboard, on the warehouse, in the quarter the warehouse supports it - and the roadmap shows exactly which quarter that is.
  • "The competitor already has AI agents." The competitor's ladder is invisible from outside; their rework bill is too. Answer: point at P10 on the roadmap, sitting behind its named enablers. Ambition is scheduled, not denied.
  • "Can't we buy a shortcut?" Sometimes - a managed platform can compress P1. But bought rungs still get climbed in order; money changes the ladder's speed, never its sequence.
Real world

The quarter that saved three. Northgate's CFO pushes hard to start forecasting (P7) in Q1 - revenue season looms. The head of data walks the ladder backwards from P7: it trains on warehouse tables that do not exist until P2 lands, which needs the landing zone from P1. Starting P7 in Q1 means data scientists idling against missing tables for two quarters at full salary. The CFO, shown the idle-cost math instead of an architecture lecture, moves the ask to Q3 himself - and becomes the roadmap's loudest defender.

Self-studyWhat genuinely runs in parallel3 min read▶

The ladder constrains the spine, not everything. Two tests decide whether projects can truly run side by side: no shared dependency (neither waits on the other's output) and no shared people (separate teams, honestly staffed - a shared ML engineer makes "parallel" a fiction). At Northgate:

  • Genuinely parallel in Q1: P1 landing zone (platform engineer + data engineers), P9 governance foundations (governance lead), P4 exec scorecard v1 on existing reports (BI team). Different rungs, different people.
  • Fake parallel: P6 segmentation "alongside" P2 lakehouse - the data scientists would spend the quarter waiting on tables. Parallel on the slide, sequential in reality, demoralizing in person.

Leader's rule of thumb: every "parallel" claim on the roadmap must name its separate team. If two bars share people, they are one queue wearing two colors.

Part 2 · covers the quarter-by-quarter year-one roadmap

Year one, quarter by quarter 8 min live

The shape of a healthy year one: Q1 buys the platform (landing zone, lakehouse underway, governance foundations, a scorecard v1 for early credibility), Q2 finishes the floor (warehouse live, reporting starts flowing), Q3 fans out (segmentation, forecasting, self-serve BI, and the agentic pilot all start - because now the platform can hold them), Q4 harvests (data product v1, the pilot's evals, the story for year two's budget). The fan-out in Q3 looks like a bet on speed; it is actually the dividend from Q1's patience.

LiveReading the strip like a sponsor4 min▶

Four things a leader should be able to read off this one picture in a steering meeting:

  • The spine. P1 into P2 into P3 - the dark chain. Anything that slips here slips everything above it. This is where your best people and your earliest attention go.
  • Q1's quiet win. The scorecard v1 ships on existing reports while the platform builds underneath. It buys the credibility that funds the boring quarters - never leave Q1 with nothing visible.
  • The Q3 gate. Five starts in one quarter is only sane if the warehouse actually landed in Q2. The Q2 exit review is therefore the most important meeting of the year: it decides whether Q3 is a fan-out or a pile-up.
  • The orange square. P10's start marker is a condition, not a date: governance landed. If P9 slips, P10 slides with it - visibly, on the next version of this page.
One page or it did not happen. If the year does not fit on one strip an executive can absorb in ninety seconds, the roadmap will be ignored in favor of hallway promises - which then become the real roadmap, unversioned and unstaffed.
Part 3 · covers versioning, MoSCoW + WSJF at portfolio level

A roadmap is a versioned contract 6 min live

A roadmap that silently changes is worse than no roadmap - people keep planning against the version in their heads. Treat yours like a contract: version it (v1.0 at approval, v1.1 when anything moves), date it (every copy says when it was true), and show the trade-offs (every change ships with what moved, what it displaced, and why). The moment someone quotes an old version at you and you can answer "that was v1.2, here is v1.4 and the change log," the roadmap has become the single source of truth - which is the whole game.

LiveMoSCoW and WSJF at portfolio altitude4 min▶

Two frameworks earn a place at this level, each doing one job:

  • MoSCoW (must / should / could / won't) sets the year's scope honestly. The list that matters most is the won't: written, visible, signed. Northgate's year-one won't list keeps real-time streaming and a second GenAI use case off the table - which is why the ten funded projects stay staffed.
  • WSJF (cost of delay divided by job size, from SAFe) sequences inside the freedom the ladder leaves. When segmentation and self-serve BI both want the same Q3 start slot, WSJF turns the fight into arithmetic the steering group can argue about in the open.
  • The ladder still vetoes both. A must-have that stands on an unbuilt rung is a must-have with a later start date - it keeps its priority and gets its enabler named, never a quiet re-score.
Real world

The change log that ended a feud. Mid-year, Northgate's warehouse slips three weeks and reporting's start moves with it. The PMO ships roadmap v1.3 the same day: what moved, why, and the downstream effect on self-serve BI. The CMO, who learned about the last such slip through the grapevine, reads the log and asks one question instead of escalating to the CEO. Trust in a roadmap is not built by never slipping - it is built by never slipping silently.

Self-studyVersion hygiene in five lines2 min read▶

The minimum viable contract, kept by the PMO: (1) the strip itself carries a version number and a date in its corner; (2) changes land only through steering, never through hallway agreement; (3) every version's change log states what moved, what it displaced, and why; (4) old versions stay retrievable - being able to show the history IS the credibility; (5) quarterly re-plan is scheduled, so change has a legitimate door and stops picking the locks.

Build-along

Break a dependency on purpose ★ 20 min · everyone traces

The fastest way to respect the ladder is to watch it fail safely. Your table takes the roadmap and deliberately ships dashboards before data validation exists - then traces the blast radius, hop by hop.

Make the swap. On the Q2-Q3 strip, pull P3's dashboards forward so they ship before the validation service (part of P3's own foundation) is running. It looks great on the slide: value one quarter early.

Trace hop one. Unvalidated pipelines feed the dashboards. Numbers render beautifully and nobody can vouch for them. Note who notices first - usually a store manager whose region looks wrong.

Trace hop two. The exec scorecard (P4) reads the same tables. Now the error sits in front of the CFO with the data office's logo on it. Estimate the trust cost in steering-meeting terms.

Trace hop three. Segmentation and forecasting (P6, P7) train on data the office now knows is suspect. Every model result gets a mental asterisk. The one-quarter head start has become a multi-project credibility tax.

Write the rule. Restore the roadmap, then write the one-sentence policy your own organization would need to stop this swap being made under pressure. Compare sentences across tables - the best ones name a person, not a principle.

Wrong numbers beat no numbers at spreading. A missing dashboard generates a complaint; a wrong dashboard generates decisions. That asymmetry is the entire case for validation before visibility.
Before next session

Homework ◐ 30 min

  • Draft your own organization's four-quarter strip: ten bars or fewer, spine marked, every parallel claim annotated with its separate team.
  • Find one dependency your current plan quietly violates (dashboards before validation is the classic - look there first). Trace its blast radius three hops, in writing.
  • Stamp your draft v0.1 with today's date and write its first change-log entry. Bring the won't list you had to write to make it fit on one page.
Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · The CMO wants self-serve BI live in Q1. What does the roadmap answer?

The ladder decides: BI reads from the warehouse, the warehouse lands with P2 in Q2, so P5 starts Q3. The ask is scheduled with its enabler visible - never refused outright and never quietly promised early.

2 · In WSJF, what sits in the numerator - the thing that makes waiting expensive?

WSJF = cost of delay / job size (SAFe). The numerator prices the waiting, the denominator prices the doing - so small items whose absence bleeds money outrank big exciting ones whose value can wait.

3 · The warehouse slips three weeks and reporting moves with it. What does a contract-grade roadmap do?

Version it, date it, show the trade-offs. Silent edits leave people planning against the version in their heads; a visible change log is how a roadmap earns the right to be believed after its first slip.
