Part 1 · covers milestone decomposition + the dependency register

Milestones and the wires between them 7 min live

A charter promises an outcome; a timeline needs milestones - binary, sponsor-legible checkpoints roughly 4-6 weeks apart. "Warehouse 60% done" is not a milestone; "store sales queryable in the warehouse" is. Then wire them: schema design blocks the dictionary, the dictionary blocks validation - and validation lives in a DIFFERENT project. Cross-project dependencies are where portfolios die quietly, because nobody's status report owns them. The PMO does.

LiveDecompose before you schedule5 min▶

Three tests for a milestone worth putting on the wall:

  • Binary. Done or not done - no percentages. "Raw extracts land daily" either happens or it does not.
  • Sponsor-legible. Written in business language. The sponsor should never need a translator to know why a milestone matters.
  • 4-6 weeks apart. Closer and you are micromanaging; further and a slip hides for a quarter before anyone sees it.

Then build the dependency register: for every milestone, what must finish first, and does that thing live inside this project or in another one? Internal dependencies get managed at stand-up. Cross-project dependencies get a named owner and a line on the portfolio wall - because the dictionary team does not attend the reporting team's stand-up.

Real world

The dependency nobody owned. Northgate's reporting lead plans validation for month 9, assuming the dictionary is done. The lakehouse lead, under pressure, quietly deprioritizes the dictionary - it is nobody's dashboard, after all. Month 9 arrives: validation cannot certify a single metric because half the fields have no agreed definition. Two projects, both "on track" in their own status reports, jointly late. The fix costs one line on the portfolio wall: "Dictionary → Validation, owner: PMO, check monthly." That line is tonight's whole lesson.

Self-studyThe dependency register: four columns, no drama3 min read▶

Keep it boring and visible: what blocks what · internal or cross-project · named owner · next check date. Two habits make it work:

  • Review it at portfolio cadence, not project cadence. Cross-project wires belong to the PMO's monthly review - the one meeting where both sides of every wire sit at the same table (steering mechanics arrive in b6).
  • Promote noisy wires. Any dependency that slips twice gets promoted to a milestone of its own on the receiving project's plan. Visibility is the cure; the register is just the symptom log.

Northgate's year one has a handful of these wires, and one of them - dictionary to validation - sits on the critical path. Which brings us to Part 2.

Part 2 · covers CPM, float, protecting the path

The critical path: find it, protect it 8 min live

The critical path method (CPM, developed at DuPont in the late 1950s) says something almost embarrassingly simple: the longest dependent chain of tasks sets the earliest possible finish. Everything on that chain has zero float - slip a week there and the whole portfolio finishes a week later. Everything off it has float: room to slip without moving the end date. The PMO's daily question is not "is everything on track?" but "is the PATH on track?"

LiveManaging by path: two different playbooks4 min▶
Task is...Your playbookExample
On the critical pathBest people, daily visibility, clear blockers within hours, never share its staffWarehouse modeling months 6-8 - the whole year funnels through it
Off the path, small floatWatch weekly - float burns fast and quietlyData dump with one month of slack
Off the path, big floatSchedule flexibly, lend its people to path tasks when neededDictionary with a four-month window before validation needs it
Newly ON the pathThe path moves when tasks slip - recompute it monthly, do not assumeIf the lake slips 2 months, the dictionary's float evaporates
The path is a computation, not an opinion. When Northgate's platform engineer says "the lake feels like the risky bit," check the math: feelings nominate candidates, the longest chain decides. And recompute after every slip - yesterday's float is not a fact about today.
Self-studyFloat is a budget, not a cushion3 min read▶

Float belongs to the portfolio, not to the task that happens to have it. Three rules keep it honest:

  • Spend float deliberately. Moving the dictionary two months later to free an engineer for the warehouse is a good trade - made on purpose, logged in the register.
  • Never let float hide a problem. A task quietly consuming its own float is a slip in disguise. Track "float remaining" the way b9 tracks budget remaining.
  • Zero float = escalation trigger. The moment a task's float hits zero, it joins the critical path and inherits the path playbook. That transition is a steering-level event, not a footnote.

b5's simulator makes this visceral: the projects that blow up are rarely the ones on the path at kickoff - they are the ones that drifted onto it while everyone watched something else.

Part 3 · covers the planning fallacy, reference classes, discovery spikes

Estimating data work without lying 6 min live

Every duration on that timeline is a guess - and human guesses are biased in one direction. The planning fallacy (Kahneman + Tversky, 1979) is the finding that people systematically underestimate their own task durations, even when they know their past estimates ran over. The fix is not trying harder: it is the outside view - anchor on how long comparable work actually took, then adjust. Data work adds a twist software does not have: discovery risk. Sometimes the data simply is not there, or is not usable - and no estimate survives that.

LiveInside view vs outside view4 min▶
QuestionInside view (default, biased)Outside view (reference class)
Warehouse build?"We know the model, 2 months""Our last three modeling efforts each took 3 months - start at 3"
Schema design?"The team is strong, 6 weeks""Comparable schema work here has run 8-10 weeks - plan 2 months"
Anything with 'just'?"Just wire up the extracts"The word 'just' is an estimate smell - go find the reference class

Mechanics: pick 3-5 comparable past efforts (yours, or a colleague's - anyone's actuals beat your intentions), take their actual durations as the anchor, and adjust only for named, specific differences. Where no reference class exists, that itself is information: you are in discovery territory, and discovery gets a spike, not an estimate.

Real world

The data dump that turned out unusable. Month 2, Northgate's engineers open the first big extract from the legacy POS system (service #6) and find the thing every data team eventually finds: timestamps in three formats, store IDs that changed in a 2019 migration nobody documented, and returns logged as negative sales in some regions but not others. The dump "landed on time" - and is unusable for the lake build (#7) and warehouse (#8) downstream. The schedule did not fail because the team was slow; it failed because the estimate assumed the data was fine. Discovery risk is not a risk you estimate away - it is one you buy information about, early and cheaply.

Self-studyDiscovery spikes: buy the information first3 min read▶

A discovery spike is a short, timeboxed investigation run BEFORE you commit a schedule for data-touching milestones: pull a real sample from every source system, profile it, and answer one question - "is this data usable for what we promised?" Rules:

  • Timeboxed, not open-ended. One to two weeks, then a decision - never "we are still investigating."
  • Front of the plan. Northgate runs the source-data spike in month 1, in parallel with schema design (#4) - so the month-2 surprise above becomes a month-1 known.
  • Output is a decision, not a report. Green: estimate holds. Amber: re-baseline the affected chain now, honestly (re-baselining etiquette lands in b5). Red: escalate scope - maybe the 2019-and-earlier history is out of scope, and the charter's out-of-scope box (b1) grows a line.

Spikes cost days and save quarters. The alternative - discovering discovery risk on the critical path in month 6 - is how "the data project is late again" becomes a company proverb.

Build-along

Build the 12-month Northgate timeline ★ 22 min · everyone builds

Input: the a2 quarter-level roadmap and your b1 charters. Output: a month-level timeline with milestones, wired dependencies, a computed critical path, and spikes where discovery risk lives.

Decompose the lakehouse. Break P2 into 5-7 milestones using the three tests (binary, sponsor-legible, 4-6 weeks apart): extracts landing, lake zones live, schema signed off, dictionary complete, warehouse marts queryable.

Wire the register. For each milestone, list what blocks it - and flag every wire that crosses a project boundary. You should find dictionary → validation. If you did not, look again: it is the one that bites.

Walk the chains. Add durations, trace every dependent chain start to finish, and mark the longest one in orange. Say out loud what the finish date is - and which five tasks own it.

Apply the outside view. Take your two scariest estimates and replace the gut number with a reference-class anchor: how long did comparable work ACTUALLY take, here or anywhere? Watch the timeline move right - that movement is the planning fallacy leaving your plan.

Place the spikes. Every milestone touching an unknown source system gets a 1-2 week discovery spike in month 1. Mark what each spike de-risks, and what its amber outcome would re-baseline.

Present the path, not the plan. When you walk this into steering, lead with the orange chain and its finish date. Forty tasks is noise; five tasks that own the year is a decision-ready picture.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · What makes a chain of tasks THE critical path?

CPM (DuPont, late 1950s): the critical path is a computation, not a judgment call. Longest dependent chain = earliest finish. Risk and executive attention often live elsewhere - which is exactly why the path needs computing rather than guessing.

2 · The dictionary has four months of float. What does that float mean?

Float is slippage room on off-path tasks only - the critical path has zero, always. And float is a portfolio budget: spend it deliberately, track what remains, and treat float hitting zero as the escalation it is.

3 · Best defense against the planning fallacy when estimating the warehouse build?

Kahneman + Tversky (1979): we systematically underestimate our own durations - the fix is the outside view. Blind doubling is padding without information; committing to optimism is the fallacy with extra steps.
