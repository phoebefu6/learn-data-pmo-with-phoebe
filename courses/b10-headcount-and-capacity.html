Part 1 · covers the focus factor + the allocation matrix

Fourteen names, ten real FTE 8 min live

The math that saves the year: 14 FTE x ~70% focus factor ≈ 10 real project FTE. The missing 30% is not laziness - it is standups, steering prep, support tickets, training, holidays, and the ad-hoc requests b7 taught you to route through intake. Plan against 14 and every project is secretly understaffed by design. Plan against 10 and the plan survives contact with the calendar. Then write the 10 down in an allocation matrix - people down the side, projects across the top, each cell a percentage of that person's focus-adjusted capacity.

LiveReading the matrix - and fixing the hotspot5 min▶

Three reads, in order, every time you open the matrix:

  • Row totals first. Anything over 100% is a promise the calendar cannot keep. Northgate's ML engineer: segmentation 40 + forecasting 50 + agentic pilot 40 = 130%. The a4 leader session calls this by name: three projects per ML engineer is a plan to fail.
  • Single points of failure second. Any project whose critical skill lives in exactly one row is one resignation away from red. The pilot depends entirely on the same overloaded ML engineer - two problems, one cell.
  • Empty capacity last. Under-100 rows are your only free lever. Northgate's data scientists carry segmentation domain knowledge - they can absorb model work without a hiring cycle.

The fix menu, cheapest first: rebalance (data scientists take segmentation features), resequence (the pilot moves to Q3 - the b1 kanban already parked it there for the governance dependency; now capacity agrees), train (a BI engineer starts AI literacy, Part 2's move), and only then hire or contract.

Real world

The 130% nobody decided. No one at Northgate ever chose to overload the ML engineer. Segmentation asked in January, forecasting in February, the pilot in March - each ask reasonable, each said yes to separately, no matrix anywhere to add them up. That is the quiet way every data team drowns: not one bad decision, but three good ones with no row total. The matrix does not prevent the asks; it makes the arithmetic public before the burnout does.

Self-studyWhy 70% - and why your number might differ3 min read▶

The 70% focus factor is a planning default, not a law of physics. It absorbs the recurring skeleton from b7 (standups, steering, sprint events, variance review), support and on-call, holidays and training, and the intake-routed ad-hoc work that is real work even when it is not project work. Two honest adjustments:

  • Measure yours once. Two weeks of coarse time tracking (project / run / overhead, nothing finer) gives you a real number. Teams carrying heavy support duty often plan at 60%; a greenfield team with no legacy can touch 80%.
  • Never plan at 100%, ever. A plan at 100% has zero slack for the discovery risk b2 taught (the unusable data dump) - so the first surprise cascades through every row. The 30% is not waste; it is the shock absorber.
Part 2 · covers the capacity gap decision + training as a multiplier

Three ways to buy capacity 6 min live

When the matrix shows a gap that rebalancing and resequencing cannot close, you have three purchases available - and they buy different things on different clocks. Hire buys permanent capability, slowly and with a ramp-up tax. Contract buys speed on a defined skill, expensively and temporarily. Train buys leverage: it makes the people you already have worth more - which is why Northgate's catalog lists data literacy training (#20) and AI literacy training (#27) as portfolio services, not HR perks.

LiveThe decision table + the Brooks tax4 min▶
Signal from the matrixReach forWatch out for
Gap is permanent - the run book needs this skill foreverHireRamp-up: months before a new hire is a full cell in the matrix - and never mid-crisis (below)
Gap is sharp + temporary - one project, one specialist skillContractKnowledge walks out at the end - pair them with a staff member or the capability leaves too
Gap is broad + shallow - many people each need a bit more skillTrain (#20 / #27)Training time IS capacity spend - put it in the matrix as a cell, or it silently eats the 70%

And the caution that guards the whole table - Brooks's law (The Mythical Man-Month, 1975): "adding manpower to a late software project makes it later." Two mechanisms: new people need ramp-up time from exactly the people who have none to give, and communication paths grow as n(n-1)/2 - a team of 5 has 10 paths, a team of 8 has 28. You met this as the simulator's CRASH anti-lever in b5: adding headcount to late projects improved nothing and added about a month plus 15% cost to every late project. Hire for the NEXT project, never to rescue this one.

Real world

The multiplier nobody budgets for. Northgate's store-ops analysts used to file a dashboard ticket for every question - each one a slice of an analytics engineer's 70%. After the data literacy course (#20), most of those questions became self-serve lookups in the BI layer from b6. Nobody's row total changed, but the intake queue thinned - which is capacity you never had to hire. Training is the only lever in this session that makes OTHER rows cheaper.

Self-studyCosting the ramp honestly2 min read▶

A new hire is not +1.0 FTE on day one. Plan the ramp as matrix cells: a fraction of a cell in the first months (they absorb more help than they produce), climbing toward full capacity over a quarter or more depending on stack complexity - and the mentor's row LOSES a slice for the same period. Budget both sides or the matrix lies for a quarter. Same logic, smaller scale, for contractors (onboarding, access, context) and for internal moves between domains. The only free-ish ramp is training an adjacent skill into someone who already knows your data - which is why train beats hire for broad-shallow gaps.

Part 3 · the capstone: six artifacts, one pack

Assemble the year-one PMO pack 6 min live

Ten sessions ago Northgate handed you a 30-line service catalog and a job title. Everything since has been building one thing: a portfolio a new Head of Data could pick up cold and RUN. That thing is the pack - six artifacts, each earned in a session, each answering one executive question. Tonight you staple them together and check the seams.

LiveWhat each artifact proves - and the health lens check4 min▶
ArtifactBuilt inThe executive question it answers
Roadmap + timelineb2 (on a2's sequence)"What lands when, and what blocks what?"
RACIb4"Who owns each deliverable?" - exactly one A, every row
Budget + buffer logb9"What does it cost, and how will we know if that changes?"
Capacity planb10"Can the people we have actually do this?" - no row over 100
Steering deckb6"How do we stay informed without micromanaging?"
Health scorecarda6 / b5"How do we know it is working - before it is not?"

Then the seam check: run the assembled pack past the a6 health lens - on-time %, budget variance, benefit realization, risk burn-down. Every lens metric must be COMPUTABLE from the pack alone. If the scorecard promises benefit realization but no charter in the pack names a benefit with a number, you found a seam - fix the charter, not the scorecard.

The pack is a system, not a stack. The roadmap's dates assume the capacity plan's rows; the budget's EAC feeds the scorecard's variance; the steering deck reports all of it. Change one artifact and touch its neighbors - that discipline is the difference between a PMO and a folder of PDFs.
Build-along

Fill the matrix, ship the pack ★ 24 min · everyone builds

The final build-along. You leave with a completed allocation matrix and the six-artifact checklist scored against the health lens - the capstone deliverable of the course.

Compute real capacity. 14 FTE, minus Head of Data and PMO lead (oversight, not delivery), times the ~70% focus factor. Write the number at the top of the matrix - it is the portfolio's true people-budget.

Fill the matrix. Allocate the six delivery rows across the 10 projects from the roadmap's current quarter. Every cell is a percentage; every promise from b1's charters must appear as a cell somewhere.

Hunt the hotspot. Total every row. Find the ML engineer's 130%. Fix it with the cheapest-first menu: rebalance to the data scientists, slip the pilot to Q3, nominate the AI-literacy trainee - and only propose a hire if the gap survives all three.

Assemble the pack. Lay out your six artifacts from b2, b4, b6, b9, b10, and a6/b5. Tick each against the checklist. A missing artifact is homework, not shame - but it goes on the list with a date.

Run the health lens. For each of a6's four metrics, point at the exact artifact and field it will be computed from. Four clean traces = the pack ships. Hand it to whoever plays your Head of Data, and shake on it.

Do this with your REAL portfolio within two weeks. The Northgate pack is the rehearsal. The version with your projects, your names, and your numbers is the one that changes your Monday - and every template you need is in sessions b1 through b10.
After the course

Homework ◐ 45 min

  • Build the allocation matrix for your real team - real names, real projects, real row totals. Find your 130% (there is almost always one) and write the cheapest-first fix before you show anyone.
  • Assemble your own six-artifact pack. Score each artifact: exists / draft / missing. Anything missing gets a session number from this course and a date on your calendar.
  • Book the recurring skeleton that keeps the pack alive: monthly variance review (b9), monthly steering (b6), quarterly re-rank (b1). A pack without a cadence is a snapshot; with one, it is a PMO.
Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Northgate has 14 FTE. How much project capacity does the portfolio actually have to plan against?

14 x 0.7 ≈ 10. The 30% is real work (cadences, support, training, ad-hoc) - it just is not project work. Planning at 14 understaffs every project by design; planning at crunch is how you lose the 14.

2 · The forecasting project is late. Leadership offers two extra engineers to catch up. Brooks's law says?

"Adding manpower to a late software project makes it later." The sim's CRASH anti-lever showed it: no on-time improvement, about a month plus 15% cost added to every late project. New capacity helps the next project, not the burning one.

3 · A new Head of Data asks for the year-one PMO pack. What lands on their desk?

All six, stapled: b2 roadmap, b4 RACI, b9 budget, b10 capacity, b6 steering deck, a6/b5 scorecard. Each answers one executive question, and the health lens must be computable from the pack alone - that is the completeness test.
