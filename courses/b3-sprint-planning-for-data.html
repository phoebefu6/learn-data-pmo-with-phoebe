Part 1 · covers Scrum Guide 2020: accountabilities, events, artifacts

Scrum 2020, straight from the source 7 min live

Forget what your last employer called Scrum - the Scrum Guide 2020 (Schwaber + Sutherland) fits on a napkin. Three accountabilities: Product Owner, Scrum Master, Developers. Five events: the Sprint itself (the container, one month or less), Sprint Planning, Daily Scrum, Sprint Review, Sprint Retrospective. Three artifacts, each carrying a commitment: Product Backlog → Product Goal, Sprint Backlog → Sprint Goal, Increment → Definition of Done. That is the whole machine. Everything else is house style.

LiveThe commitments are the point5 min▶

Teams memorize the events and forget the commitments - but the commitments are what make the artifacts mean something:

  • Product Goal (on the Product Backlog) - the long-range "why" the backlog exists. For Northgate's reporting project: "every store manager reads their own numbers without filing a ticket."
  • Sprint Goal (on the Sprint Backlog) - ONE sentence the sprint stands or falls on. Not a list. If the team finishes eight tickets and misses the goal, the sprint failed; if they hit the goal and drop two tickets, it succeeded.
  • Definition of Done (on the Increment) - the quality bar that decides whether work counts AT ALL. Undone work does not ship in a data team any more than in a software team - it ships as a dashboard nobody trusts.

And the sizing rule that matters most for the PMO: sprints are one month or less. Northgate runs two weeks - short enough that a slipping project slips visibly in b5's simulator terms: early, cheap, fixable.

Real world

The sprint with no goal. Northgate's first attempt at sprint planning produces a "goal" that reads: "finish tickets 12 through 23." Two weeks later, eleven tickets are done, one is not - and nobody can say whether the sprint worked, because a list is not a goal. Take two: "a store manager in the pilot region opens the sales dashboard and the numbers match finance." Suddenly the team self-organizes - the validation ticket jumps the queue, two nice-to-have tickets quietly wait. One sentence did what no burndown chart ever has.

Self-studyWhat the PMO does with Scrum (and does not)3 min read▶

Notice what is missing from the accountabilities: there is no "project manager" in the Scrum Guide. That is not a problem - it is a boundary. The PMO does not run the Daily Scrum, assign tickets, or attend every event with a clipboard. What the PMO does:

  • Feeds planning. The b2 timeline and dependency register tell each sprint planning what the portfolio needs next - the critical path shows up as backlog order, not as pressure in the room.
  • Reads reviews. The Sprint Review is the PMO's best early-warning instrument: real increments, real feedback, every two weeks. Slip shows up here a quarter before it shows up in a status deck.
  • Protects the cadence. When an exec wants to inject "one quick thing" mid-sprint, that request routes through intake (b1) and waits for planning - the PMO absorbs the interrupt so the team does not (more in b7).
Part 2 · covers estimable vs iterative work, CRISP-DM, definition of done per type

Why data sprints differ 8 min live

Here is the split that saves your sprints: pipeline and app-dev work estimates like software - known steps, stable sizes, story points hold. DS/ML work iterates like CRISP-DM (1999): business understanding, data understanding, data preparation, modeling, evaluation, deployment - six phases drawn as a LOOP, not a line, because evaluation routinely sends you back to the start. You cannot story-point a loop. You timebox it: commit to two weeks of learning, then decide - and never promise an accuracy number as if it were a feature.

LiveDefinition of done, per work type5 min▶

One team, one Definition of Done? Not in a data team. "Done" is real for every work type - but it is a DIFFERENT sentence for each:

Work typeDone meansDone does NOT mean
Pipeline / app dev (#2)Deployed, tested, monitored, documented - runs without its author watching"Works on my machine" or a demo from a branch
Dashboard (#11)Published, numbers validated against source, an owner named, a store manager has used it"Looks right" - unvalidated dashboards are rumors with charts
Model (#22, #23)Deployed and monitored, meeting the metric bar AGREED WITH THE BUSINESS before modeling startedA great number in a notebook - notebooks are evaluation, not deployment
Research spike (#21)The timebox ended AND a written decision exists: proceed, pivot, or stop"We are still exploring" - a spike without a decision just burned its box
Set the model's metric bar before modeling starts. Agree it with the business in phase 1 (business understanding), write it into the DoD, and evaluation becomes a checkpoint instead of a negotiation. A bar set after the fact always drifts to wherever the model landed.
Self-studyTimeboxing research: commit to learning, not to accuracy3 min read▶

The honest way to put DS/ML work in a sprint:

  • Frame the spike as a question. Not "build the segmentation model" but "can transaction history separate Northgate's shoppers into actionable segments? Two weeks, answer required."
  • The timebox is the estimate. The commitment is the two weeks and the decision at the end - never the result. Model risk (the accuracy may never reach the bar) is a feature of the work, not a planning failure.
  • Re-box deliberately, at planning. If the spike earns another loop through CRISP-DM, that is a new timeboxed item pulled at the NEXT sprint planning - in daylight, priced against the rest of the backlog. Quiet rollovers are how "a two-week look" becomes a two-quarter science project.

This is the same honesty rail b5's simulator enforces: the sales forecasting project carries irreducible model risk no lever removes - the win is an early, honest re-baseline, never a pretended green.

Part 3 · covers sprint goals across app dev, reporting, forecasting, segmentation

One backlog, four kinds of work 5 min live

Northgate's team is 14 people, not four teams of 14 - so application development (#2), smart reporting (#10) and dashboarding (#11), sales forecasting (#22) and ML (#23), and customer segmentation (#21) all draw from one backlog, ordered by ONE Product Owner using the b1 scores and the b2 critical path. The craft is writing a sprint goal for each work type without pretending they are the same kind of promise.

LiveFour sprint goals, side by side4 min▶
Domain workA real sprint goalWhat kind of promise
App dev (#2)"The store-ops intake app accepts and routes requests end to end"Delivery - estimable, points hold
Reporting + dashboards (#10, #11)"Pilot-region managers see validated daily sales in the dashboard"Delivery - estimable, validation included
Forecasting + ML (#22, #23)"We know whether weekly store-level forecasts can beat the current manual method - decision documented"Learning - timeboxed, ends in a decision
Segmentation (#21)"Three candidate segmentations profiled, one recommended to the CMO"Learning - timeboxed, output is a recommendation

Notice both learning goals are still binary - you can say yes or no at review. That is the bar: a goal you cannot verify at the Sprint Review is a wish, whatever lane it came from.

Real world

Mixed-lane planning, the hard way. In an early sprint, Northgate's planning fills the sprint entirely with estimable reporting tickets - they size so cleanly, they always win the queue. The forecasting spike waits "one more sprint" three times. Meanwhile the b2 timeline quietly assumed forecasting discovery started in month 4. The fix is structural, not motivational: the Product Owner reserves capacity for the iterative lane every sprint - Northgate settles around a rough 70/30 split - so learning work cannot be perpetually outbid by work that merely estimates better.

Build-along

Plan sprint 1 for the reporting project ★ 22 min · everyone builds

Input: the P3 reporting backlog, the b2 timeline, a two-week sprint. Output: a sprint goal, a sized Sprint Backlog, and a commitment the team actually believes.

Write the sprint goal first. One sentence, verifiable at review: "a pilot store manager opens the sales dashboard and the numbers match finance." Everything pulled next must serve it.

Pull from the backlog. Take the top ordered items that serve the goal: source queries, dashboard build, the validation checks (#9) the b2 dependency register says must come first. Skip anything that does not serve the goal, however tempting its size.

Size the estimable items. Story points on the pipeline and dashboard tickets - relative sizing against work the team has done, not hopes. Any ticket containing the word "just" gets re-examined (b2's estimate smell applies here too).

Box the unknowns. The one genuinely uncertain item - "will the POS extracts refresh fast enough for daily numbers?" - becomes a timeboxed spike with a decision due at review, not a padded estimate.

Commit and check capacity. Sum against real capacity - the 3 analytics engineers minus meetings, support, and the focus factor (the full capacity math lands in b10). Then ask the only commitment question that matters: "do WE believe this goal fits in two weeks?" Adjust until the answer is yes.

Protect sprint 1 from ambition. The first sprint sets the team's belief about what commitment means. A modest goal hit beats a bold goal missed - you can raise ambition in sprint 2; rebuilding trust takes five.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · In the Scrum Guide 2020, which commitment belongs to the Sprint Backlog?

Each artifact carries exactly one commitment: Product Backlog → Product Goal, Sprint Backlog → Sprint Goal, Increment → Definition of Done. The pairing matters - the Sprint Goal is what makes a sprint more than a ticket list.

2 · Why does the segmentation research get a timebox instead of a story-point estimate?

You cannot reliably size a loop: the iterative lane carries model and discovery risk that points cannot capture. The timebox caps the spend, and its deliverable is a decision - proceed, pivot, or stop - which IS verifiable at review.

3 · Which is a real Definition of Done for the sales forecasting model?

A model is done when it is in production, watched, and clearing a bar the business agreed to in advance. Notebook accuracy is the evaluation phase of CRISP-DM - two phases short of deployed - and "feels finished" is not verifiable by anyone.
