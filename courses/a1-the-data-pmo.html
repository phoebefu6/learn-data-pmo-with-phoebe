Part 1 · covers project vs program vs portfolio

Three levels, three questions 7 min live

A project is one team shipping one deliverable against one charter - the question it answers is "is this piece on track?" A program is a set of related projects steered toward one outcome - "do the pieces add up?" A portfolio is every bet the office is making, ranked on one list against finite money and people - "are these the right bets at all?" A data office with 30 requested services and 14 people lives or dies at the portfolio level: the hardest word in year one is not "how" but "not yet".

LiveWhy a data office needs portfolio thinking on day one5 min▶

Most organizations discover portfolio thinking late, after two years of project-by-project chaos. A data office cannot afford that, for three structural reasons:

  • Demand always exceeds capacity. Northgate's catalog holds 30 services; 14 FTE can honestly run about 10 projects. Without a ranked list, the loudest sponsor wins - which is a ranking method, just a terrible one.
  • Everything shares the same platform. The forecasting model, the dashboards, and the agent pilot all stand on the same lake and warehouse. Project-level thinking optimizes each piece; only the portfolio sees that they share one foundation and one queue.
  • The money is one pot. $2,700k of change budget across 10 projects means every yes to one thing is a quiet no to another. Portfolio thinking makes the trade visible before it is made, not after.
Real world

The menu problem. Northgate's first steering meeting, week two: the CMO circles "Customer Segmentation" in the catalog, the CFO circles "Executive Scorecard", store ops circles "Sales Forecasting". Three circles, three verbal yeses, zero sequencing. By week six all three are "in progress" and none is staffed above half a team. The catalog was written to show what the office CAN do - read as a menu of simultaneous orders, it quietly booked the same 14 people three times. The portfolio lens exists to catch exactly this.

Self-studyWhere programs fit - and when to skip them3 min read▶

Programs earn their overhead when several projects must land together to release any value - a migration plus a retraining plus a cutover, say. Northgate's year one does not have that shape: its 10 projects release value one at a time, in dependency order. So the portfolio steers projects directly, and "program manager" is a hat nobody wears yet.

The leader's test: add a level only when the level below keeps producing decisions nobody is placed to make. If two projects keep fighting over one outcome, that is a program smell. If ten projects keep fighting over one budget, that is the portfolio - and that one you need from day one.

Part 2 · covers the 4-domain catalog + the dependency reality

Four domains, one direction 8 min live

Northgate's 30 services group into four domains: Infrastructure (8 services - cloud, lake, warehouse), Data Analytics (11 - reports, KPIs, BI), Data Science (5 - segments, forecasts), and Gen AI (7 - governance, agents, as Northgate files them). The domains are not four aisles you shop from in any order. They stack: infrastructure feeds analytics, analytics feeds data science, data science feeds Gen AI. The stack is a physical dependency, not an opinion - and it is the single most useful fact a leader can hold when a board member asks why the agent pilot is not first.

LiveReading the stack like a sponsor4 min▶

Three leader-level consequences fall straight out of the stack:

  • Early quarters look boring on purpose. Landing zone and lakehouse produce no dashboard anyone applauds - they produce the floor the applause lines stand on. Budget your patience along with your money.
  • The exciting stuff is hostage to the dull stuff. Forecasting quality is capped by warehouse quality; agent behavior is capped by governance quality. When a model underwhelms, look one lane down before blaming the data scientists.
  • Governance is not a lane of its own - it underpins all four. Northgate files Data Governance and the Data Quality Scorecard under Gen AI, because agents forced the issue - but the discipline pays off in every lane above the lake.
Real world

The upside-down year. A Northgate board member returns from a conference and asks for "an AI agent for store managers, this quarter." The head of data does not argue AI - she draws the stack: the agent reads from the warehouse, the warehouse does not exist yet, and an agent on ungoverned data automates mistakes at machine speed. The ask goes on the portfolio as P10 with its enablers named, scheduled behind governance. Same ambition, right order - and the board member becomes the pilot's sponsor instead of its casualty.

Self-studyWhy 30 services and not 30 projects3 min read▶

The catalog's 30 lines roll up into 10 portfolio projects - services sharing a team, a dependency chain, and a sponsor become one project with one charter, one Accountable owner, one budget line. Data Lake, Data Warehouse, Schema Design, Data Dictionary, and Data Dump are five catalog lines and exactly one project: the lakehouse build. Thirty separately chartered projects would drown 14 people in coordination overhead; four domain-blobs would be too coarse to steer.

You will do this rollup yourself in tonight's demo. The Practitioner track's b1 works the same rollup from the delivery side, with the charter template and the scoring math.

Part 3 · covers what the PMO owns, what it never owns, PMBOK 7

What your PMO does - and must not do 7 min live

A data PMO owns exactly four things: cadence (the meeting and reporting rhythm that makes progress visible), visibility (one honest wall showing every project's state), escalation (problems reach the right decider while they are still cheap), and money (budget tracking and variance, not budget setting). It must never build things, own products, or make architecture calls - the moment it does, it stops being the honest broker and becomes one more team defending its own work. Guard that line personally; it erodes from both sides.

LivePMBOK 7's eight performance domains - the leader's checklist5 min▶

PMBOK Guide 7th edition (PMI, 2021) replaced 49 processes with 12 principles and 8 performance domains. Skip the certification framing; as a leader, read the domains as eight questions to ask your PMO lead in the first month. If any question has no owner, you found the gap before it found you.

Self-studyThe two failure modes of a new PMO3 min read▶

Failure mode 1: the PMO becomes a build team. It starts innocently - the PMO lead "just helps" with a dashboard, then owns it, then defends its priority in the very steering meeting they are supposed to broker. Cure: the PMO's headcount plan contains zero builders, forever.

Failure mode 2: the PMO becomes a reporting theater. Beautiful status decks, no escalations, no declined requests, every project green until the month it turns red. Cure: judge your PMO by decisions surfaced per month, not slides produced. A PMO that never brings you a hard call is not running a portfolio - it is decorating one.

Build-along

Sort the 30 services into 10 projects ★ 20 min · everyone sorts

The full Northgate catalog on cards - 30 services across four domains. Your table plays the steering committee and builds the portfolio.

Deal the catalog. Spread all 30 service cards. First pass: sort into the four domains (Infrastructure 8, Analytics 11, Data Science 5, Gen AI 7 - Northgate files governance under Gen AI; keep their grouping).

Roll up. Group services that share a team, a dependency chain, and a sponsor into candidate projects. Target ten-ish; argue the edge cases out loud. Is Data Literacy Training its own project or part of segmentation's adoption plan? (Northgate: part of it.)

Compare to Northgate's answer. P1 landing zone, P2 lakehouse, P3 reporting, P4 scorecard + KPI, P5 self-serve BI, P6 segmentation, P7 forecasting, P8 data product, P9 governance + DQ, P10 agentic pilot. Where you differ, decide whose rollup survives a budget review better - there is more than one defensible answer.

Stack it. Arrange the 10 projects on the four-lane ladder. Mark every project whose lane sits above an unbuilt lane - that is your "not yet" list, and saying it out loud now is the whole point.

Name one A each. For each project, propose exactly one Accountable owner from the 14-FTE org. Notice where you wanted two - that itch is next session's material, and b4 on the Practitioner track works the full RACI.

Keep the disagreements. Where your table's rollup differed from Northgate's, write the difference down - it is the best predictor of where your real organization's portfolio will fight itself.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · What question does the portfolio level answer that no project can?

Projects answer "on track?", programs answer "adds up?", the portfolio answers "right bets?" - one ranked list against one pot of money and 14 people. A data office with 30 requested services dies without that question having an owner.

2 · Northgate's 10 projects each get exactly one Accountable owner. Why one, not two?

The one-A rule (from RACI: one and only one Accountable per deliverable) exists because shared accountability evaporates exactly when it is needed - when the project is in trouble. Full RACI mechanics land in a4 and Practitioner b4.

3 · Which of these does a healthy data PMO own?

The PMO owns cadence, visibility, escalation, and money. The moment it builds things, owns products, or makes architecture calls, it becomes one more team defending its own work - and you lose your honest broker.
