Part 1 · covers intake, charters, the 30-into-10 rollup

The catalog is not a plan 7 min live

A service catalog answers "what can the data office do?" A portfolio answers "what will we do this year, in what order, with whose money?" The PMO's first job is the translation. The move: group services that share a team, a dependency chain, and a sponsor into ONE project with one charter, one owner, one budget line. Northgate's 30 services collapse into 10 projects - and suddenly the year is legible.

LiveThe one-page charter5 min▶

A charter is not paperwork - it is the cheapest conflict-prevention tool in the PMO kit. One page, seven boxes, signed before work starts:

  • Why - the business outcome in one sentence, with a number ("cut month-end reporting from 9 days to 2").
  • What - in scope / out of scope, each 3 bullets max. Out-of-scope is the box that saves you in month 4.
  • Who - sponsor, Accountable owner (exactly one), key Consulted parties. Full RACI arrives in b4.
  • When - target quarter and the 2-3 milestones that matter to the sponsor, not the 40 that matter to you.
  • How much - budget line plus the assumption it rests on ("4 engineers at 70% focus").
  • Risks - top 3, each with the early-warning signal you will watch.
  • Done means - the acceptance test in business language ("store managers pull their own P&L without a ticket").
Real world

The out-of-scope box earns its keep. Northgate's lakehouse charter says "out of scope: real-time streaming, historical data before 2022, the legacy ERP's custom tables." In month 4 a director asks for streaming "since you're in there anyway." The PM points at the signed page: happy to charter it as new work with new money. Conversation over in two minutes - because it happened on paper in month 0.

Self-studyIntake board: where requests go so they stop ambushing you3 min read▶

Every ask enters through ONE door - a visible intake board with five states: Received → Sized → Scored → Scheduled → Declined-with-reasons. Two rules make it work:

  • Nothing skips the board. The CFO's "quick question" and the intern's dashboard idea take the same door. Exceptions become the norm within a month - the board dies quietly.
  • Declined is a first-class state. A visible, reasoned no ("below the WSJF cut line, revisit in Q3") builds more trust than a silent maybe. Ad-hoc thematic analysis requests especially: route them here, not through DMs (more in b7).

Decision rights, written down: the PMO runs the board, the Head of Data owns the cut line, the steering committee re-ranks quarterly. Anyone can submit; nobody can queue-jump.

Part 2 · covers MoSCoW, RICE, WSJF on the Northgate backlog

Scoring that ends arguments 8 min live

Prioritization frameworks do not find the right answer - they make the disagreement visible and specific, which is the actual goal. Three tools, three jobs: MoSCoW (must / should / could / won't) for scope conversations inside one project. RICE (Reach x Impact x Confidence / Effort) when you have usage data and many small candidates. WSJF (cost of delay / job size) when sequencing big items against time - which is exactly the portfolio problem.

The score is the conversation, not the verdict. When Northgate's CMO scores segmentation's cost of delay as 8 and Finance scores it 3, the number is doing its job: the disagreement is now specific, on the table, and resolvable in one steering agenda item - instead of leaking out as month-long passive resistance.
Self-studyDependency beats score: the one override that is always legal3 min read▶

One caveat before you fall in love with any scoring model: dependencies outrank scores. Northgate's self-serve BI enablement might out-score the lakehouse on pure value - but self-serve BI on top of no governed warehouse is self-serve chaos. The dependency ladder (infra feeds analytics feeds DS feeds Gen AI) is a hard constraint, not another opinion to weigh. b2 makes this precise with the critical path; a2 on the Leader track shows the same ladder from the roadmap chair.

Practical rule: score everything, THEN lay the top scorers against the dependency ladder, and let the ladder veto. A high-value project that cannot start yet goes on the roadmap with its enabler named - visible, sequenced, unblocked on schedule - rather than being quietly re-scored downward.

Part 3 · covers the portfolio kanban + who decides

Who decides - written down 5 min live

Most portfolio dysfunction is not bad prioritization - it is ambiguous decision rights. Three questions, three named answers, published where everyone can see them: Who can add to the queue? (Anyone - through the intake board.) Who ranks it? (The PMO scores, steering ranks, quarterly.) Who can say stop? (The Head of Data kills or pauses, steering confirms.) Write these down in week one and half of b8's conflict sessions never happen.

LiveThe portfolio kanban - one wall, whole year4 min▶

Five columns, ten cards, one glance: Intake · Chartered · In flight · Delivered · Parked. Each card carries exactly four facts: Accountable owner, target quarter, budget line, RAG status. Everything else lives one click deeper.

  • In-flight limit. Northgate caps in-flight at 6 of 10 - because 14 people at ~70% focus is about 10 real FTE (the math lands in b10). More in flight = everything slower.
  • Parked is honest. The agentic pilot sits in Parked with a note: "starts Q3, after governance lands." Visible waiting beats invisible promising.
  • The wall is the status report. If the kanban is current, Monday status meetings shrink to exceptions only (b5 shows what "current" costs and pays).
Real world

The quiet queue-jump test. Month 2: a regional VP emails the lead engineer directly - "small favor, one dashboard." The engineer forwards it to intake, the PMO cards it, it scores below the line, steering declines it with the reason attached. The VP grumbles once. The alternative - the favor economy - costs Northgate its in-flight limit within a quarter, invisibly.

Build-along

Charter the lakehouse, score the queue ★ 22 min · everyone builds

Real material only: your own team's request list if you brought one, Northgate's catalog if not. You leave with a filled charter and a scored queue.

Roll up. Take the 30-service catalog and group into candidate projects: shared team + shared dependency chain + shared sponsor = one project. Compare against Northgate's 10 - where you differ, argue it out loud (there is more than one defensible rollup).

Charter one. Fill the seven boxes for the lakehouse build: why (with a number), in/out of scope, sponsor + one Accountable owner, target quarter + 3 milestones, $480k + the staffing assumption, top 3 risks with early-warning signals, done-means.

Stress the out-of-scope box. Partner plays the month-4 director asking for streaming. Practice the two-minute answer: point at the page, offer a new charter with new money.

Score three projects with WSJF. Lakehouse, exec scorecard, agentic pilot - cost of delay and job size on the 1-2-3-5-8 scale, scored by the table, not by one voice. Watch the scorecard outrank the pilot.

Apply the dependency veto. Lay your top scorers against the infra-to-GenAI ladder. Anything blocked gets its enabler named and a start quarter - not a silent downgrade.

Keep the charter to one page. The moment it spills to two, executives stop reading it and it stops preventing conflicts. Ruthless is a feature.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · What turns 30 catalog services into 10 portfolio projects?

The rollup rule: shared team + shared dependencies + shared sponsor = one project. Four domain-projects would be too coarse to charter honestly; 30 projects would drown 14 people in overhead.

2 · WSJF ranks Northgate's "boring" exec scorecard above the exciting agentic pilot. What drove that?

WSJF = cost of delay / job size. The scorecard's delay cost (5) against a lean job size (3) beats the pilot's 3/3. Hype is not an input to the formula - that is the point of using it.

3 · A high-WSJF project depends on the unfinished warehouse. What does the PMO do?

Dependencies outrank scores, but the veto must be visible: the project keeps its score, gets its enabler named, and waits on the roadmap in daylight - never a quiet re-score.
