Part 1 · covers the power x interest grid + the exec scorecard (#12) and KPI system (#15) as stakeholder artifacts

Map first, then plan - the grid decides the comms 7 min live

Stakeholder management fails when it is improvised per person: the loud get demos, the quiet get forgotten, and the CFO learns about a slip from a corridor. The fix is mechanical. Place every stakeholder on a power x interest grid, and let the quadrant dictate the comms: manage the high-power high-interest closely, keep the high-power low-interest satisfied with zero surprises, keep the high-interest informed generously, and monitor the rest without guilt. The plan falls straight out of the map.

★ What you walk out with today A filled power x interest grid for Northgate's five key stakeholders, a comms plan with a written row per quadrant, a monthly trust-cadence skeleton you can lay on your own calendar, and the discovery-workshop agenda that aligns executives before a single agent gets built.
LiveThe scorecard is a stakeholder artifact, not a reporting chore4 min▶

Two catalog services do double duty as your stakeholder machinery, and treating them that way changes how you build them:

  • Executive scorecard (#12) - this is what "manage closely" LOOKS like in practice. The CFO does not want your Gantt chart; they want the same one-page scorecard every month: on-time status, budget variance, the two decisions steering owes. Build it for the reader in the top-right quadrant, and the steering meeting stops being show-and-tell.
  • KPI system design (#15) - the quiet trust-builder. When "gross margin" means one thing on every dashboard (definitions locked in b4's metric RACI), executives stop cross-examining the numbers and start discussing them. Contested definitions are the number-one way data teams burn stakeholder trust without noticing.
Real world

Bad news travels first class. Month 5: the forecasting project needs a re-baseline (the b5 story). The PMO walks it to the CFO two days BEFORE steering - one page, cause, two options, a recommendation. In steering the CFO backs the re-baseline before anyone else speaks. The alternative timeline - the CFO ambushed by the same slide in public - produces a defensive meeting and a suspicious quarter. Same news, opposite trust outcome. Quadrant top-right earns the early call: that is what "manage closely" means.

Self-studyReading the grid without being cynical about it3 min read▶

The grid can feel Machiavellian - it is not, if you use it as an attention budget rather than a ranking of human worth. Three honest reads:

  • "Keep satisfied" is respect, not neglect. Legal and IT do not want more meetings from you. They want no surprises and early consultation on the sensitive stuff. Flooding them with demos ANNOYS the high-power low-interest quadrant - that is the classic rookie move.
  • "Monitor" is not "ignore". It is a lightweight honest channel plus watchfulness: a store analyst who starts pulling your data weekly is migrating quadrants, and your plan should notice before they do.
  • Placement is a hypothesis. You will misplace someone. The quarterly re-map is where the grid gets corrected - which is why it stays private and dated, like any working document.
Part 2 · covers steering + demos + insights sharing (#18), self-serve BI (#13), biz performance monitoring (#17)

Cadences that build trust 7 min live

Stakeholder trust is not built in big moments - it compounds on repetition. The same scorecard, the same day, every month, for a year: that is what makes a data office feel reliable. Northgate's skeleton has four beats: a weekly PMO review (internal), a sprint demo every two weeks (open invite), a monthly steering that decides, and a monthly insights share that DELIVERS. Under it all, an always-on layer: live business performance monitoring (#17), so meetings discuss the numbers instead of reading them out.

Run insights sharing as a product, not a favor. Same slot every month, one finding, one recommended action, one owner - and track whether anyone acts on it. A digest nobody acts on for three straight months is telling you to change the finding, the audience, or both. That feedback loop is what makes it a product.
Real world

The demo that recruited a sponsor. Northgate's biweekly demo is open invite - and in month 3 a store ops regional manager wandered in to watch the dashboarding work. She saw her own region's numbers render live, asked two sharp questions, and left with the digest subscription. By the discovery workshop in Part 3 she was the loudest voice scoring the store-workflow use case - a sponsor the data team never had to pitch. Demos do quiet recruiting that steering packs never will: that is why the invite stays open even when attendance is thin.

Self-studySelf-serve BI (#13): the enablement that kills the fire drill3 min read▶

Every Friday-afternoon "quick number for Monday" request is a stakeholder telling you they have no other way to get an answer. You can staff for the fire drills forever, or you can remove their cause. Self-serve BI enablement is stakeholder management by architecture:

  • Curated, not open. Self-serve on governed, defined metrics (the #15 system) is empowerment; self-serve on raw tables is chaos with extra steps. The b1 lesson stands: enablement rides ON the governed layer.
  • Teach the top-right quadrant's teams first. When the CFO's analyst can self-serve, the CFO's ad-hoc requests fall - and the top-right quadrant is where your attention budget is most expensive.
  • Route what remains through intake. Ad-hoc thematic analysis (#16) still exists for the genuinely novel questions - but it enters through the b1 intake board, not through a DM. The full time-defense playbook lands in b7.
Real world

The fire drill is a symptom, not a personality. Northgate's store ops kept requesting the same three cuts of weekly sales - each time as an urgent one-off. The team's instinct was to grumble about "stakeholders who can't plan". The comms plan said otherwise: high interest, no self-serve path. One enablement session and a pinned dashboard later, the repeat requests became self-serve pulls, and the requests still arriving were the interesting ones. The grumble was misdiagnosed demand.

Part 3 · covers the AI use case discovery workshop (#28) as stakeholder alignment

Discovery workshops - align before you automate 5 min live

The agentic pilot (P10) is where stakeholder management gets tested hardest, because everyone has an AI idea and most of them should not be built. The AI use case discovery workshop (#28) is a stakeholder-alignment tool wearing a technical costume: get the executives in one room BEFORE any build, inventory the real pains, and rank them together - so the pilot that gets built carries their fingerprints, not just the data team's enthusiasm.

LiveThe workshop that picks problems worth automating4 min▶

Half a day, the right people, four moves:

  • Inventory pains, not solutions. Open with "what eats your team's week?" - not "where could we use AI?". The second question produces demo-day fantasies; the first produces automatable drudgery.
  • Score together: value x feasibility. Business stakeholders score value, the data team scores feasibility (is the data there? is the workflow stable?). Scoring in the same room is the alignment - the argument about a score IS the requirements conversation.
  • Rank, and keep the losers visible. The output is a ranked problem list with a named owner per item - winners feed the b1 intake board like any other work; the rest stay parked in daylight.
  • Say no to the pet use case out loud. Every workshop has one exec favorite that scores poorly. Declining it WITH the scores on the wall, in the room, is cheaper than declining it by email forever after.
The workshop output is alignment, not a backlog. The ranked list matters less than the fact that the CFO, CMO, and store ops watched each other agree to it. When the pilot hits its first rough sprint, that shared memory is what keeps the sponsors sponsoring.
Self-studyWhat "feasibility" means when the data team scores it3 min read▶

Value is the business side's column; feasibility is yours - and scoring it honestly in front of executives is a stakeholder move in itself. Three questions behind the number:

  • Is the data actually there? Data work carries discovery risk that app work does not: the source might be missing, stale, or unusable. A use case whose data you have never touched gets a low confidence score, said out loud - not a hopeful shrug.
  • Is the workflow stable? Automating a process that changes every month means rebuilding the automation every month. Boring, stable, high-volume drudgery is the sweet spot - which is why the winning use case is rarely the glamorous one.
  • Can we tell if it works? If nobody can define what a good output looks like, nobody can evaluate the agent producing it. Flag it in the room; the evaluation mechanics arrive with the pilot's cost story in b9.

Scoring low on feasibility is not saying no to the stakeholder - it is showing your homework. Executives forgive "not yet, and here is why" far more readily than a confident yes that dies in month three.

Build-along

Write the comms plan rows - CFO and store ops ★ 22 min · everyone builds

One comms plan, one row per stakeholder, six columns. You write two rows tonight - the top-right quadrant's most expensive reader and the bottom-right's most numerous one - then compare against the worked versions below.

Place them on the grid. CFO: high power, high interest (funds the portfolio, chairs steering) - manage closely. Store ops managers: high interest, lower power (live with every dashboard daily) - keep informed. Say the quadrant out loud before writing anything; the quadrant dictates the row.

Choose the artifact per reader. CFO: the exec scorecard (#12) plus the steering pack - decisions and variance, one page. Store ops: the demo invite plus the monthly insights digest (#18) - things they can use this week, not portfolio finance.

Set frequency and channel. CFO: scorecard monthly two days before steering, slip alerts within a day, in person for anything amber. Store ops: demo every two weeks, digest monthly, self-serve dashboard (#13) always on for the routine numbers.

Write the "never hears late" clause. The sharpest column in the plan. CFO: never hears about a budget overrun or re-baseline second-hand. Store ops: never discovers a metric definition changed by seeing their number move.

Name the owner and stress-test it. Every row has one owner (PMO lead for the CFO row; the analytics lead for store ops). Then run the b5 scenario against both rows: forecasting slips - who calls whom, in what order, before steering meets?

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Legal is high power, low interest. The team proposes adding them to every biweekly demo "to keep them engaged". Good move?

Each quadrant gets the comms it wants, not the comms you enjoy giving. High-power low-interest stakeholders want no surprises and respect for their time - demos are for the high-interest quadrants. And "only when something goes wrong" guarantees every contact is a bad one.

2 · What separates a demo from a status meeting?

Demos show, steering decides, and status meetings got retired by always-on business performance monitoring (#17). The moment slides replace the real thing in a demo, it has quietly become a status meeting with better lighting.

3 · What is the primary output of the AI use case discovery workshop (#28)?

The workshop picks problems worth automating BEFORE anything gets built - and the ranking's real value is that the executives produced it together. Winners then enter through the b1 intake board like any other work; nothing skips the door.
