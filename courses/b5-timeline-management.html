Part 1 · covers slip-early tracking, App Performance Monitoring + Data Validation as early warning

Status that predicts, not describes 8 min live

Most status reports are history lessons: "we did X last week." A PMO report is a forecast: "at this burn and this velocity, we land in March, not January - decide now." The difference is WHEN a slip becomes visible. A slip surfaced in month 2 costs a conversation and a re-plan. The same slip surfaced in month 6 costs money already burned, stakeholders already promised, and options already gone. Everything in this session exists to move discovery left.

LiveThree early-warning instruments a data portfolio already has5 min▶

Data offices are lucky: the portfolio produces its own telemetry. Wire these three into the weekly review and slips announce themselves:

  • App performance monitoring (service #3) - pipeline run times, failure rates, queue depth. A nightly load that crept from 40 minutes to 3 hours is a schedule slip that has not landed yet.
  • Data validation (service #9) - reconciliation pass rates. Dashboards blocked on failing validation are late BEFORE the dashboard workstream misses anything.
  • Sprint velocity trend (b3) - three sprints of declining completion on one project is a forecast, not a blip. Ask what it predicts, not who to blame.
SPI thinking without the ceremony. Full earned-value lands in b9. The habit to install tonight is the ratio in your head: planned-by-now vs done-by-now. Below 1.0 and trending down = say it out loud this week. The number is less important than the reflex.
Self-studyWhy data projects slip differently3 min read▶

Application projects mostly slip on scope and estimation. Data projects add two slip modes of their own, and your tracking has to watch for both:

  • Discovery risk - "the data is not there / not usable." The b2 estimate assumed the ERP's order table was clean; week 3 finds the data dump has three years of duplicate keys. Fix: spike early (b2), track spike outcomes as first-class status.
  • Model risk - the forecasting model may plateau below the accuracy the business needs, and no amount of schedule pressure changes the math. CRISP-DM is a loop, not a line - which is why b3 timeboxes iteration and why one project on tonight's board can never be forced green.

Read the ladder the way an exec would: RACI, sprints, and steering are schedule levers. Capacity is the biggest single jump (6 → 9). The buffer is a money lever - it never rescues a date, it rescues the year-end number.

LiveThe red button: Brooks's law, one click4 min▶

"Crash it: add headcount to late projects" is the instinct every steering committee has in month 6. The simulator lets you try it. Result: on-time delivery never improves, every late project gets one month later, and the overrun jumps (at zero levers: +38% becomes +61%).

Why, mechanically: new people need ramp-up from exactly the people who are busiest, and communication paths grow as n(n-1)/2 - a team of 4 has 6 paths, a team of 8 has 28. Fred Brooks wrote it in The Mythical Man-Month in 1975: "adding manpower to a late software project makes it later." Half a century of managers have re-verified it the expensive way.

Real world

What TO do with money in month 6: buy scope relief (descope to the must-haves), buy dependency relief (a contractor on the UPSTREAM blocker, who ramps up on isolated work), or buy the NEXT project's start. Headcount on the late project itself is the one purchase that reliably makes things worse.

Self-studyHow the simulator computes - and what is honest about it3 min read▶

Each project carries scripted sensitivities: months of slip that occur while a given lever is off, drawn from real failure patterns (unowned source systems, scope churn, exec surprises, double-booked specialists, unmetered cloud and token spend). The arithmetic on top is computed live in your browser: late months burn at 60% of the project's monthly rate, missing buffers add the documented overrun, crashing adds 15% of budget plus ramp-up burn per late project. It is a teaching simulation, honestly labeled - the patterns are scripted, the math is real, and the numbers above are its exact canon.

And the deliberate imperfection: 9 of 10 is the ceiling. The sales forecasting model carries irreducible model risk - no process lever makes a hard prediction problem easy. What the levers change is HOW it lands: unmanaged, it is a month-12 surprise; with sprints + steering on, it becomes an amber "re-baselined" - v1 ships two SKU families, v2 goes on the roadmap, and steering heard it in month 3. A PMO's promise is not zero slips. It is zero surprises.

Part 3 · covers honest re-baselining, Sales Forecasting + Data Quality Scorecard as the monitoring pair

Re-baselining without losing the room 6 min live

A re-baseline is not an admission of failure - hiding one is. The test of a good re-baseline is that steering says "thank you for telling us in March" instead of "why are we hearing this in November." Three rules make the difference.

LiveThe three rules + the script5 min▶
  • Re-baseline once, not monthly. A plan that moves every review is not a plan. Gather the slip, its cause, and the recovery options into ONE decision moment.
  • Bring options, not apologies. Descope (v1 = 2 SKU families), extend (8 months, same scope), or add upstream capacity (contractor on data prep). Each priced in time and money. Steering picks; you recommend.
  • Show what the monitoring caught. "The data quality scorecard flagged SKU-level sparsity in week 3; the model plateau confirmed it by sprint 4" - evidence that the system works builds trust even while the news is bad.
★ The re-baseline opener - steal this Forecasting update, decision needed today. The model reliably hits the accuracy bar for beverages and snacks - not yet for fresh, and the data will not support fresh this year (quality scorecard evidence attached). Options: A ship v1 on two families in March and charter v2 for fresh next year - my recommendation; B extend 3 months for one more data-prep cycle at $65k, 40% confidence; C stop now, keep the pipeline for BI. All three keep the March dashboard commitment. Which way?
Build-along

Drive the ladder yourself ★ 22 min · everyone builds

Solo first, then pairs. The goal is not to memorize the numbers - it is to be able to NAME the missing lever from the failure story, which is exactly the diagnostic skill real portfolios need.

Baseline. All levers off. Read every red row's note - each one is a failure story. Write down which of the five disciplines each story is missing before touching anything.

Climb the ladder left to right, one lever at a time. After each click, find WHICH projects changed and check it against your prediction. Note the 6 → 9 jump at capacity - the biggest single rung.

Press CRASH at zero levers. Watch +38% become +61% with zero schedule gain. Then turn all five levers on and press it again - even on a healthy portfolio it still hurts (the one amber project gets later). Say the n(n-1)/2 reason out loud to your pair.

Find the ceiling. All levers on: who is still not green? Read the forecasting row's amber note. Pair-practice the re-baseline script on it - one of you is the CFO who wanted all-SKU forecasts.

Map to your world. Each person names their real portfolio's weakest lever and the project it is currently costing. That lever's session (b3/b4/b6/b9/b10) is your homework priority.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · At zero levers the portfolio runs +38% over budget. You press "Crash it: add headcount to late projects." What happens?

Brooks's law, live: ramp-up steals time from the busiest people and communication paths grow as n(n-1)/2. The simulator prices it at +1 month and +15% budget per late project - generous compared to some real cases.

2 · With all five levers on, the sales forecasting project shows amber "re-baselined," never green. Why is that the intended lesson?

No process lever makes a hard prediction problem easy. The levers change WHEN you find out and HOW it lands: v1 ships two SKU families in March with steering's blessing, instead of an all-SKU promise dying in November. Zero surprises, not zero slips.

3 · The nightly pipeline load crept from 40 minutes to 3 hours, but every workstream still reports green. What is the PMO read?

The portfolio's own telemetry (APM, validation pass rates, velocity trend) predicts slips before any workstream misses a date. Moving discovery left is the entire economics of tracking - month-2 slips cost conversations, month-6 slips cost money and trust.
