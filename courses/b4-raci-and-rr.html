One deliverable, exactly one A

Northgate's governance project sat still for two months on one unanswered question: "whose data is this?" Nobody was wrong - nobody was accountable. Tonight you learn the grid that prevents that stall: RACI done properly (R does the work, exactly ONE A owns the outcome, C is two-way, I is one-way), the three smells that tell you a grid is decorative, four worked RACIs - one per Northgate domain - and the R&R layer beyond the grid: decision rights and escalation lanes, so a stuck question always has somewhere to go next.

Part 1 · covers the four letters, the one-A rule, the three smells

RACI mechanics - four letters, one hard rule 7 min live

RACI is a per-deliverable grid, not a per-project org chart. Each row is one deliverable; each cell says what one role does about it. Responsible does the work - several Rs is fine. Accountable owns the outcome - exactly ONE per deliverable, always, and it is a named person, not "the team". Consulted is two-way: their input arrives BEFORE the call and can change it. Informed is one-way: they are told AFTER. Most broken grids break exactly one of those four definitions.

★ What you walk out with today Four worked RACIs covering all four Northgate domains, a 60-second smell test you can run on any grid someone hands you, and the escalation-lane picture that would have saved the governance project its two lost months.
LiveThe one-A rule, and why A + R together is legal4 min▶

The one-A rule is the entire value of RACI compressed into one constraint: for every deliverable there is exactly one person who answers for the outcome. Two clarifications that save endless grid arguments:

  • A + R in one cell is legal. The lead data engineer can both do the schema work and own its outcome. Two As in one ROW is never legal - that is not shared ownership, it is pre-negotiated blame diffusion.
  • The A is a name, not a box. "Data engineering" cannot be accountable. "Priya, lead data engineer" can. If you cannot write a name in the cell, you have found the gap before it found you.
  • C earns its letter. A real C's input can change the decision, and it arrives before the decision. If their input cannot change anything, they are an I - move them and win the time back.
Real world

The two-month stall, in one row. Northgate's governance project (p9) had a RACI - a decorative one. "Domain data ownership" had an empty A: everyone assumed the governance lead owned it, the governance lead assumed the domains did. When the first real call arrived ("whose data is customer email - marketing's or the warehouse's?") the question circled meetings for two months. The fix took one afternoon: a name in the A cell per domain, and an escalation lane for ties. The lesson: the grid did not fail - the empty cell did.

Self-studyRACI is per deliverable, not per project3 min read▶

The most common RACI mistake is drawing one grid for the whole project: "lakehouse build - A: platform engineer." That is an org chart wearing a costume. Ownership shifts per deliverable: the schema's A is not the dictionary's A, and the dictionary's A is not the validation sign-off's A. Draw the rows at the level where decisions actually get made.

  • Too coarse ("the project": 1 row) - the grid answers no real question anyone asks mid-project.
  • Too fine ("every Jira ticket": 400 rows) - nobody maintains it past week two, so it silently lies.
  • Right altitude: the deliverables on the charter plus the recurring decisions (change approval, access, publish). Northgate's grids below run 4-5 rows each - that is deliberate, not lazy.

Part 3 · covers decision rights, escalation lanes, governance lead vs stewards

R&R beyond the grid - where stuck questions go 6 min live

A RACI tells you who owns each deliverable. It does not tell you what happens when two As disagree, or when a question arrives that no row anticipated - and "whose data is this?" was exactly such a question. That needs the second layer: decision rights (who decides what class of question) and an escalation lane (where a stuck question goes next, and how fast). Write both down in week one; they are three sentences each.

LiveGovernance lead vs domain stewards - the split that scales4 min▶

One person cannot own the correctness of every field in a 120-store retailer - and should not try. The split that works:

  • The governance lead owns the standard: what a definition looks like, what a quality rule looks like, how access decisions get recorded. One person, one rulebook, all domains.
  • Domain stewards own their domain within the standard: the merchandising steward answers for merchandising data, store ops for store data. They are business people close to the data's meaning, not engineers close to its plumbing.
  • Ties go up, fast. When two stewards both claim (or both refuse) a dataset, the governance lead tie-breaks on the standard; if it is genuinely cross-domain or political, it rides the lane up to the Head of Data with options attached. One working session per lane - a stuck question is aging inventory.
Real world

Escalation is a service, not a failure. After the fix, Northgate's stewards started escalating small ownership questions within days instead of hoarding them - because the lane made it cheap and blame-free. The count of stuck questions went up for a month, which looked bad and was actually the backlog finally becoming visible. Then it fell. If your escalation lane is never used, it is not because nothing is stuck.

Self-studyDecision rights in three sentences - the b1 questions, one level down3 min read▶

b1 wrote decision rights for the portfolio (who submits, who ranks, who kills). The same discipline applies inside each project, and it is three sentences per project, not a policy document:

  • Who decides day to day? The row's A, within the charter's scope. If the A has to ask permission for routine calls, the grid is decorative and the real A is hiding one lane up.
  • Who tie-breaks? Named in advance, per class of question - technical ties to the lead, standards ties to the governance lead, scope ties to the sponsor. A tie-break invented during the tie is a negotiation, not a rule.
  • What escalates automatically? Cross-project impact, money beyond the charter, anything a steward and the governance lead cannot settle in one session. Automatic beats heroic: nobody should need courage to escalate.

Write the three sentences on the same page as the RACI. A grid tells you who owns the known deliverables; the sentences catch the questions nobody predicted - which is exactly the kind that stalled p9.

Build-along

Fix the broken RACI - the governance stall ★ 22 min · everyone builds

You get the grid Northgate actually had when p9 stalled - two As on the policy row, an empty A on ownership, ten Cs on the DQ rules. Your job: make it decide things again.

Run the 60-second smell test. Scan row by row: count the As per row (must be exactly one), count the Cs (would each one's input change the call?), find rows where work exists but no A does. Mark every smell before touching anything.

Fix the two-A row. Policy showed the governance lead AND the IT director as A. Decide who answers for the outcome, demote the other to C - and say out loud why: two As means the first miss becomes a blame negotiation.

Fill the empty A. "Domain ownership map" had no A. Give it to the Head of Data, then cascade: each domain row gets a NAMED steward as A. If the table cannot produce a name for a domain, you have found the real gap - log it as an action, not a guess.

Cut the Cs from ten to three. For each C on the DQ-rules row ask one question: can their input change the rule? No = they become I. Watch how much lighter the row gets - that weight was your cycle time.

Add the lane. Write the three-lane escalation picture next to the grid: stewards decide their domain, governance lead tie-breaks on the standard, Head of Data takes cross-domain calls - one working session per lane, options attached. Then replay "whose data is customer email?" and time it. Two months becomes two lanes.

Fix the grid WITH the people in it, not about them. A RACI revised in private reads as a power move and gets quietly ignored. Twenty minutes at a whiteboard with the named people beats a perfect grid nobody signed.
Before next session

Homework ◐ 30 min

  • Draw the RACI for ONE real deliverable on your team - 4-5 rows, real names in the A cells. If any A cell resists getting a name, that resistance is your finding: bring it.
  • Run the 60-second smell test on a grid you did not write (a colleague's project, an old wiki page). Log the smells: how many As per row, how many Cs would actually change the call?
  • Write your escalation lane in three sentences: who decides day to day, who tie-breaks, who takes cross-cutting calls - and how long a question may sit in each lane before it moves up.
Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · The lead data engineer is both A and R for the warehouse schema. A colleague objects: "you can't hold two letters." Who is right?

One person can do the work AND own the outcome - that is common and healthy. The one-A rule constrains the row, not the person: exactly one A per deliverable, and it is a named person, not a team.

2 · Finance wants to "stay in the loop" on the dictionary scorecard but their input never changes it. Which letter, and why?

C is two-way and arrives BEFORE the decision; I is one-way and arrives after. Handing out courtesy Cs is how a row grows ten of them - and every C is a review lap your cycle time pays for.

3 · A project hums along for weeks, then freezes solid at its first real decision. Which RACI smell predicts exactly this?

The empty A is the sneakiest smell because the grid looks busy and healthy right up until a decision is needed. That is the p9 story: months of motion, then "whose data is this?" with no cell to answer it.
