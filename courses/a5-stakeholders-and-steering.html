Part 1 · covers the power x interest grid for Northgate's executives

Map the room before the room maps you 7 min live

Every stakeholder tool starts with the same 2x2: power (can they change your budget, your scope, your existence?) against interest (does your portfolio touch their week?). The grid is not a personality test - it is a routing table. It tells you who gets a seat at steering, who gets a page, who gets a newsletter, and who gets a quiet coffee before anything surprises them.

LiveThe five players, read honestly5 min▶

Placement is a judgment call you revisit quarterly - people move when the portfolio touches them. Northgate's opening read:

  • CFO - manage closely. Signs the $2,700k change budget, chairs steering. Wants variance and benefits in numbers, monthly, no adjectives.
  • CMO - manage closely. Highest-energy champion (segmentation, forecasting) and the most likely to queue-jump. Channel the energy through steering, not around it.
  • Store ops - keep informed. 120 stores of daily dashboard users. Less budget power, enormous adoption power - they decide whether anything you ship gets used.
  • IT - keep informed, treat as co-builder. Owns the cloud estate and change control. Ignore them and your landing zone waits in their queue.
  • Legal - keep satisfied. Low interest until customer data or AI shows up, then instant veto power. One early briefing beats a month-5 stop order.
Real world

The quadrant nobody briefs is the one that stops you. Northgate's segmentation project is humming until legal learns - from a lunch conversation - that customer purchase histories feed a model. Work pauses three weeks for a privacy review that would have taken three days in month 1. Nothing about the model changed; the sequence of who-heard-what did. Keep-satisfied is cheap insurance: one page, quarterly, before they need it.

Self-studyInterest is a verb: people move on the grid3 min read▶

The grid is a snapshot, and portfolios melt snapshots. Three predictable migrations to plan for:

  • Legal moves right the week the agentic pilot touches customer conversations - low interest becomes high interest overnight. Your quarterly briefing is what makes that arrival friendly.
  • Store ops move up once the exec scorecard makes store P&L visible - regional VPs discover opinions about your backlog. Route them to intake (b1) before the favor economy forms.
  • A champion can cool. If the CMO's segmentation lands late, your loudest advocate becomes your loudest critic. Manage-closely means bad news travels to them first, from you.

Practical habit: re-place the grid at the quarterly board meeting. It takes ten minutes and it is the cheapest strategic review the portfolio gets.

Part 2 · covers the three-loop cadence + the executive scorecard (#12) and KPI system design (#15)

The cadence that replaces panic 8 min live

Steering is not a meeting - it is a rhythm with three loops at three speeds: a weekly PMO review that filters noise into exceptions, a monthly steering committee that makes decisions, and a quarterly board that re-ranks the portfolio. The trick that makes all three cheap: they read from the SAME artifact. Northgate builds it as a real project - the executive scorecard (service #12), sitting on KPI system design (service #15) so every number has one definition and one owner.

LiveSteering vs status: the distinction that saves an hour a month5 min▶
LoopQuestion it answersOutputDeath mode
Weekly PMO reviewWhat moved off plan this week?Exception list + owner per itemBecomes a 10-project readout everyone endures
Monthly steeringWhat do the sponsors need to DECIDE?Logged decisions with options pricedBecomes status theater - information in, nothing out
Quarterly boardIs this still the right portfolio?Re-ranked queue, kills and starts confirmedBecomes a victory lap - nothing is ever re-ranked

The test for any agenda item: if nobody has to decide anything, it is status - send it as a page, not a meeting. Northgate's rule is blunt: an item gets steering airtime only if it carries a decision or an escalation. Everything else rides the scorecard pre-read.

Why the scorecard must be a project, not a slide. A deck someone assembles the night before steering will drift - numbers get "improved" en route. Service #12 makes the scorecard a governed product: KPI definitions from #15, refreshed from the warehouse, same numbers whether the CFO or an analyst opens it. Steering trust is a data quality problem before it is a communication problem.
Self-studyThe steering pack: five pages, twenty minutes of reading3 min read▶

Northgate's monthly pack, sent 48 hours ahead so the meeting starts decided-adjacent:

  • Page 1 - the scorecard. On-time, variance, benefits, risks. The a6 health view, one glance.
  • Page 2 - exceptions. Only ambers and reds, each with owner, cause, and the ask.
  • Page 3 - decisions needed. Two or three items max, each with options priced in time and money.
  • Page 4 - decisions made last time and what happened. Nothing builds trust faster than closing loops.
  • Page 5 - the horizon. What lands next month, what starts, what legal or IT should see coming.

Anyone who has read the pack can chair the meeting. That is the point - the rhythm should survive any one person's vacation, including yours.

Part 3 · covers escalation paths + the PMO as honest broker

Saying no with data, escalating without drama 6 min live

The PMO's authority is strange: almost no formal power, enormous informational power. That only works as an honest broker - the function whose numbers both sides of any fight trust. Which means the no is never "the PMO says no." It is "here is the cut line, here is the capacity math, here is what displacing project 6 costs - which trade do you want to make?" The data says no; you stay the broker.

LiveThe escalation ladder - and what triggers a climb4 min▶

One path, published in week one: project owner → weekly PMO review → monthly steering → quarterly board. An issue climbs a rung when, and only when, it needs a decision above the current rung's authority. Three honest triggers:

  • Cross-project contention - two projects need the same engineer in the same month. The PMO cannot rank sponsors against each other; steering can.
  • The triangle moved - scope, date, or money on any chartered project needs to change. That is a sponsor decision by definition (the charter is their signature).
  • A stakeholder is routing around the system - queue-jumps, side-channel builds, shadow spreadsheets. Escalate the pattern, never the person.

And the counter-rule: bad news alone is not an escalation. A slip with a recovery plan inside existing authority is a weekly-review item. Escalating everything teaches executives to stop listening; escalating precisely teaches them to lean in when you do.

Real world

The no that kept a friend. Month 3: the CMO wants a campaign-attribution dashboard "in two weeks, it's small." The PMO lead does not argue. She brings one page to steering: the WSJF score (below the cut line), the capacity math (both analytics engineers committed to the exec scorecard until month 4), and two priced options - wait eight weeks, or displace the scorecard the CFO chairs steering to get. The CMO looks at the page, picks waiting, and later tells the head of data it was the first time a no came with its reasons attached. The broker survived; so did the relationship.

Self-studyManaging up without theatrics3 min read▶

Three quiet habits that do the work heroic memos pretend to:

  • No surprises, ever. Anything that will be said in steering reaches the affected executive before the meeting. Steering confirms decisions; it should never break news to a sponsor about their own project.
  • Feed forward, not blame back. When a decision goes sideways, bring "here is what we change next month," not a post-mortem of who said what. Executives fund people who make the next decision easier.
  • Let the artifact carry the tension. A red cell on a governed scorecard starts a calmer conversation than the same fact spoken across a table. Put the awkward number in the pre-read and let everyone arrive having already had their private reaction.

None of this is spin. It is sequencing - the same truths, ordered so the room can act on them.

All four share one root: the meeting drifted from deciding to performing. The fix is always the same direction - fewer pages, fewer people, more verbs.

Build-along

Run the 20-minute steering agenda ★ 20 min · everyone chairs

Groups of four: one chair, one CFO, one CMO, one PMO lead presenting. Material: the live Northgate portfolio as of month 6. The goal is to feel how fast a decision-shaped agenda moves - and how instantly a status-shaped one bogs down.

Minutes 0-2 · silence. Scorecard on screen. Everyone reads. No narration - if the artifact needs a voiceover, the artifact failed (note what you would fix).

Minutes 2-8 · exceptions. The PMO lead walks ONLY the ambers and reds: cause, owner, ask. The chair cuts any sentence that is history rather than forecast.

Minutes 8-16 · decisions. Two queued items, each with priced options: release buffer to the lakehouse, and the CMO's attribution-dashboard request against the WSJF cut line. Sponsors must pick - "let's discuss offline" is banned.

Minutes 16-19 · escalations + horizon. One item climbing to the quarterly board (a re-rank candidate), one heads-up for legal (the agentic pilot reaches customer data next month).

Minute 19-20 · read back. The chair repeats every decision and owner out loud, and they go in the log. Then swap roles and note what the CFO chair does differently from the CMO chair.

If it fit in 20 minutes, it was steering. The meeting only ran long when someone smuggled status in through the exceptions slot. That is the diagnostic to take home: time-in-meeting is a direct measure of how much deciding versus describing you are doing.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Legal barely attends anything and never asks about the portfolio. Where do they sit on Northgate's grid, and what do they get?

Low interest does not mean low power. Legal can stop the segmentation project cold - the keep-satisfied strategy (brief early, brief lightly) is what turns a month-5 stop order into a month-1 three-day review.

2 · An agenda item for monthly steering has an update, a chart, and no question attached. What does the cadence say to do with it?

Steering exists to decide. Items without a decision or escalation ride the pre-read; the meeting spends its hour on options and choices. That single filter is what keeps the whole rhythm at 20-60 minutes instead of two hours of theater.

3 · A project slips two weeks but the owner has a recovery plan within existing scope, date, and budget authority. Escalate to steering?

The ladder climbs on authority, not anxiety: cross-project contention, triangle changes, or routing-around patterns go up; managed slips stay in the weekly loop (and on the scorecard - never hidden). Escalate everything and executives stop listening; escalate precisely and they lean in.
