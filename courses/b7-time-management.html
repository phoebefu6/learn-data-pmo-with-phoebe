Part 1 · covers maker vs manager schedules, protecting deep work

Two kinds of schedule live in one team 7 min live

Paul Graham's 2009 essay names the split: makers run on half-day units, managers run on one-hour slots. A data engineer mid-pipeline and a PM mid-stakeholder-round are not doing the same kind of time. One meeting dropped at 2pm does not cost a maker one hour - it splits the afternoon in half and can ruin it. The PMO's job is not to attend everything. It is to make sure the 14-person team keeps its half-day deep-work blocks - by putting YOUR calendar between the interrupts and theirs.

★ What you walk out with today A defended weekly calendar: the team's maker blocks marked and protected, your own week Eisenhower-sorted and timeboxed, the recurring PMO skeleton laid on five days, and a practiced 14-to-7 meeting cut where every deletion has a named trade-off.
LiveThe PM absorbs the interrupts5 min▶

Interrupts are not evil - stakeholders have real questions and the portfolio genuinely needs a human router. The design decision is where they land. If they land on makers, every ping costs a half-day unit. If they land on you, they cost one slot on a calendar already built out of slots. Three concrete moves:

  • Publish the maker blocks. Northgate blocks 9:30-12:30 for all engineers, every day, visible on the shared calendar. A published block is a norm; a private one is a preference that loses to any invite.
  • Be the front door in person. "Great question - send it to me, not to Priya" is a sentence you will say weekly. You triage in minutes what would cost her an afternoon.
  • Batch what you absorb. Collect the interrupts and bring them to the team once a day at a scheduled edge (right after daily scrum), not as they arrive. Absorbing and then forwarding in real time protects nobody.
Real world

The 2pm invite test. Month four: a well-meaning IT director invites two Northgate data engineers to a Thursday 2pm "quick sync." The PM declines on their behalf, takes the meeting solo, and brings back the one actual question - which the engineers answer in a two-line message. Cost with PM absorption: 30 PM-minutes. Cost without: two ruined maker afternoons, because a 2pm slot splits 1:30-4:30 into two stubs too short for deep work. The director never notices the substitution. The engineers do.

Self-studyMeetings at the edges: scheduling around maker time3 min read▶

When a meeting with makers is genuinely needed - sprint planning, review, retro - put it at the edge of the day, touching another meeting or the start/end of work, never in the middle of a block. Graham's core observation: for a maker, the damage of a meeting is not its duration but its position. A 30-minute meeting at 9:00 costs 30 minutes; the same meeting at 2:00 costs the afternoon.

  • Stack, don't sprinkle. One meeting-heavy afternoon per week beats five lightly-sprinkled days. Northgate stacks sprint review + retro + planning on alternating Friday afternoons.
  • Guard your own block too. The PM writes charters, timelines, and variance narratives - maker work. One 2-hour block, twice a week, defended like the team's. A PM with zero maker time ships zero artifacts.
Part 2 · covers Eisenhower, timeboxing, Parkinson's law, meeting hygiene

Your own week, defended 8 min live

The PM's week fails differently from the team's: not fragmentation but flooding - everything arrives marked urgent. The Eisenhower matrix sorts by two independent axes, urgent x important, and the whole trick is refusing to let urgency impersonate importance. Then two laws of physics for what remains: timebox it, because Parkinson's law (1955) says work expands to fill the time available - a status deck given a morning takes a morning; given 45 minutes, it takes 45 and is usually better.

LiveMeeting hygiene: agenda or decline4 min▶

One rule, applied without exceptions including to yourself: no agenda, no meeting. An agenda is three lines - decision needed, inputs attached, who must be there. If the organizer cannot write it, the meeting is not ready to exist. The polite decline: "Happy to join once there's an agenda with the decision named - or answer async if you send the question."

Default durations are Parkinson's front door. Northgate switched the calendar default from 60 to 45 minutes and nothing broke - the same decisions got made in the same meetings, 25% smaller. Nobody misses the quarter hour, and back-to-back meetings gained a breathing gap.
Self-studyTimebox patterns that fit data work3 min read▶

Timeboxing is not one trick - it is a family, and data work uses specific members of it:

  • The exploration timebox. "Two days on the data dump, then we decide" - b2's spike pattern applied to your week. Open-ended exploration is where data weeks go to die; a dated decision point converts curiosity into a plan. The output of the box is the decision, not necessarily the answer.
  • The polish timebox. Decks, dashboards, and documentation can absorb infinite refinement - Parkinson's favorite food. "The steering pack gets 45 minutes and ships at whatever state 45 minutes buys" is not sloppiness; it is a deliberate quality-cost trade you make once instead of renegotiating with yourself hourly.
  • The batch box. Email, Slack, and intake triage each get two fixed daily slots instead of ambient all-day attention. The surprise is how little urgency survives a four-hour wait - most "urgent" pings self-resolve or clarify before the batch opens.
  • The hard stop as a feature. End timeboxed meetings at the box even mid-topic, and park the remainder on the agenda for next time. Two meetings of this and the group starts front-loading what matters - the box trains the meeting.
Self-studyThe ad-hoc analysis trap (#16): DMs are a shadow intake3 min read▶

Ad-hoc thematic analysis is a legitimate catalog service - Northgate lists it as service #16. What kills teams is the delivery channel: the DM. A direct message to a data scientist is an invisible, unscored, unqueued project with an implied deadline of "now." Ten of those a week and the portfolio is running a shadow backlog nobody chartered.

  • Every ask takes the one door. The intake board from b1 - Received, Sized, Scored, Scheduled, or Declined-with-reasons. The CMO's "quick cut of the segments" goes on a card like everything else. Small asks clear fast; the point is visibility, not bureaucracy.
  • Redirect without friction. The team's standing reply is one sentence and a link: "Adding this to intake so it gets scheduled properly - here's the card." No lecture, no guilt. The requester keeps their dignity and the queue keeps its integrity.
  • Watch the pattern, not just the asks. If the same exec files five ad-hoc asks a month, that is not five requests - that is an unmet reporting need. Feed it to b6's insights-sharing cadence or the self-serve BI backlog (#13) and retire the whole class of interrupts.
Part 3 · covers the recurring skeleton: steering, sprint events, variance review, insights share

The recurring skeleton on one week 5 min live

Everything this course has installed - steering (b6), sprint events (b3), variance review (b9's preview), insights sharing (#18) - has a cadence. Laid on one week, the recurring skeleton is surprisingly small: it fits, with room for maker blocks, if and only if nothing else metastasizes. The skeleton is the calendar's constitution: recurring slots are pre-decided, everything else fights for what remains.

Count it: for the PM that is roughly seven recurring hours a week plus two monthly slots. A 14-meeting week is not the skeleton's fault - it is the skeleton plus seven meetings that never passed the agenda test.

Self-studyThe calendar is where the focus factor lives3 min read▶

b1 mentioned it and b10 will do the math: Northgate plans capacity at ~70% focus - 14 people are about 10 real FTE. The missing 30% is not laziness; it is exactly what tonight's session is about: meetings, interrupts, context switches, intake triage. Which means the calendar is not adjacent to capacity planning - it IS capacity planning, enforced daily.

  • A defended calendar keeps the 70% honest. Maker blocks published and protected, meetings at the edges, interrupts absorbed by the PM - that is what "70% focus" looks like in practice. Without the defense, real focus drifts to 50% and every b2 timeline quietly becomes fiction.
  • A rotting calendar is a leading indicator. When maker blocks start losing to invites, delivery slips follow four to six weeks later - long before any status report turns amber. Check the team's calendar in your weekly review the way b9 checks the burn: it is the cheapest early-warning signal you own.
  • Escalate calendar debt like scope debt. If a stakeholder's recurring demands push a squad below the planned focus factor, that is a capacity variance - it goes to steering with numbers ("this cadence costs 0.5 FTE"), not into quiet team suffering.

Hold that thought for b10, where the allocation matrix makes the same math explicit across all 10 projects - and the capstone pack gets a capacity page the calendar has to keep true.

Build-along

Rebuild a broken week: 14 meetings to 7 ★ 22 min · everyone builds

You get the PM's real calendar from Northgate month four: 14 meetings, zero maker blocks, engineers double-booked twice. Bring your own week instead if you dare - it is more fun and more painful. The rule of the exercise: every cut must name what it trades away. Free lunches are not on the menu; cheap ones are.

Inventory the 14. List every meeting with three facts: decision it produces, who must be there, what breaks if it vanishes for a month. No meeting is defended by "we've always had it."

Eisenhower-sort them. Do-now stays. Schedule-it moves to the skeleton. Route-it goes to intake (yes, meetings can be routed - a recurring "quick sync" about analysis asks IS service #16 wearing a calendar costume). Decline-it gets the agenda-or-async line.

Make the seven cuts, and price each one. Cancel two statuses readable from the kanban - trade: execs must actually open the kanban, so you owe them a 2-minute walkthrough. Merge two vendor calls into one - trade: the smaller vendor gets less airtime. Decline three agenda-less syncs - trade: you will absorb two of them as async threads, which costs writing time. Seven remain.

Timebox what survives. Every survivor drops from 60 to 45 or 25. Parkinson gives the time back without a fight - the same decisions fit the smaller boxes.

Lay the skeleton and pour the concrete. Place the recurring skeleton first, then the team's 9:30-12:30 maker blocks, then your own two PM blocks. What is left is the true budget for everything new next week - visible, finite, defensible.

Name the trade or the cut will not hold. A meeting cancelled with "this is waste" comes back in six weeks with a sponsor. A meeting cancelled with "we trade this for the kanban plus a walkthrough, and here is the walkthrough" stays cancelled - because the need it served still has a home.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Why does a 30-minute meeting at 2pm cost a data engineer more than 30 minutes?

Graham's maker's schedule (2009): for makers the damage of a meeting is its position, not its duration. The same 30 minutes at the edge of the day costs 30 minutes; mid-block it costs the block. That is why the PM absorbs interrupts and stacks meetings at the edges.

2 · Parkinson's law says work expands to fill the time available. What is the PMO's working counter?

Parkinson's law (1955) is beaten by shrinking the container, not padding it - buffers just give the expansion more room. Northgate's 60-to-45 default change made the same decisions happen in 25% less time. Buffers belong in budgets (b9), not in meeting slots.

3 · The CMO DMs a data scientist asking for a "quick" thematic analysis. What is the right move?

DMs are a shadow intake: unscored, unqueued, invisible work with an implied deadline of now. Service #16 is legitimate - the channel is the problem. The one-door rule from b1 holds for the CMO too, and repeat asks signal an unmet need for b6's insights cadence to retire.
