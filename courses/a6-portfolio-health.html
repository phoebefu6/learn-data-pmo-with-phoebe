Part 1 · covers the four dials + leading vs lagging indicators

Four dials, two kinds of truth 7 min live

Portfolio health fits on one page or it is not being managed: on-time % (schedule truth), budget variance (money truth), benefit realization (the only truth the board funds you for), and risk burn-down (trouble's direction of travel). But the sharper split is temporal: lagging dials report what already happened; leading dials - velocity trend, validation pass rates, pipeline run times - predict what will. A board with only lagging dials is a rear-view mirror at highway speed.

LiveHow a leader reads the four dials5 min▶

Each dial has a leader question attached - the question is the skill:

  • On-time % - not "who is late" but "what do the late ones share?" Three projects late for three reasons is noise; three late on the same shared engineer is a capacity finding (a4).
  • Budget variance - direction beats level. +17% and improving after a lever came on is a different animal from +17% and drifting. Ask for the trend line, never the snapshot.
  • Benefit realization - the dial everyone forgets after funding. If the scorecard shipped but store managers still file tickets for P&L numbers, the benefit is zero regardless of the green delivery status.
  • Risk burn-down - the cheapest leading indicator: are risks being closed faster than found? A rising count with green projects everywhere means the projects are lying, politely.
Real world

Green board, red telemetry. Month 5 at Northgate: every workstream reports green, but the leading row disagrees - validation pass rates slipping three weeks running, the nightly pipeline creeping from 40 minutes toward 3 hours. The head of data asks one question at steering: "what do the leading dials predict the lagging dials will say in month 7?" The answer surfaces the reporting project's slip two months before it would have landed as a surprise. Leading dials exist to make exactly that question askable.

Self-studyWhere the dials come from - no new bureaucracy3 min read▶

The health scorecard should cost almost nothing to refresh, because every dial is a byproduct of machinery you already funded:

  • On-time % falls out of the b2 timeline against charter dates - no separate tracking sheet.
  • Variance falls out of the a3/b9 monthly review that the buffer discipline already requires.
  • Benefits ride the biz performance monitoring service (#17) - the same KPI plumbing the business uses, pointed at your own portfolio's promises.
  • The leading row is engineering telemetry (APM, validation) plus sprint data - collected by machines, immune to optimism.

The rule of thumb: if refreshing the health board takes anyone more than an hour a month, you built a reporting project, not a scorecard - and someone will quietly stop refreshing it by Q3.

Part 2 · covers kill / pivot / re-baseline + the sales forecasting worked example

Gates: sunk cost is not a reason 7 min live

A stage gate is a scheduled moment where continuing must be justified by the future, not the past. The only legal question is "what does the NEXT dollar buy?" - never "how much have we spent?" The money already spent is gone whichever way you decide; it votes in every gate meeting anyway, through the most senior voice in the room. The gate's job is to outvote it with evidence, and the outcome is always one of three: kill, pivot, or re-baseline.

LiveThe forecasting re-baseline, from the exec chair5 min▶

Northgate's sales forecasting project is the course's honest heartbreak: the model reliably hits the accuracy bar for beverages and snacks, and will not hit it for fresh this year - the data cannot support it, and the biz performance monitoring service (#17) has the evidence on the table. The gate meeting is where leadership either earns its keep or destroys it:

  • What the room wants to say: "we have spent too much to ship half a forecast." That is the sunk cost fallacy wearing a suit.
  • What the gate says: the next dollar buys either a March v1 on two SKU families (real value, real date), a paid extension with modest confidence, or a stop. Priced options, sponsor's choice.
  • The decision: re-baseline - v1 ships two families in March, v2 for fresh goes back through intake as chartered future work. Steering heard it early, so it lands as a decision, not a scandal.

How you receive the news is a lever. If the room punishes the messenger, the next re-baseline arrives six months later and twice as expensive. The leader's line that keeps the system honest: "thank you for telling us in March."

Real world

The kill that paid for itself. The same gate discipline, different exit: a data product concept clears two gates, then the benefit case collapses when the target customer signs with a competitor's tool. The gate kills it, the pipeline work rolls into the lakehouse, and the freed engineers unblock the reporting project. Nobody calls it a failure at the quarterly board - it is presented, accurately, as $190k of remaining budget redeployed to projects with live benefit cases. Portfolios that never kill anything are not disciplined; they are just slow.

Self-studyKill vs pivot vs re-baseline - the tell for each3 min read▶
ExitThe tellWhat survives
KillThe benefit case itself died - the business changed, not the planReusable assets (pipelines, cleaned data), the team's credibility, the budget remainder
PivotThe value exists but somewhere else than chartered - wrong problem, right capabilityThe capability; the charter does not - pivots re-enter intake and get scored like new work
Re-baselineThe value and problem are right, the plan's assumptions were not (data quality, model risk, estimates)The goal and the team - only dates, scope slices, or money change, once, in daylight

The abuse to watch for: serial re-baselining as a euphemism for drift. The b5 rule holds at the exec level - re-baseline once per finding, not monthly, and every re-baseline names what the monitoring caught and when.

Part 3 · the portfolio simulator - read the board, name the missing lever, ask for the right fix

Northgate's year, on your desk ★ 20 min · everyone drives

This is the same live board practitioners drive in b5 - ten projects, $2,700k, twelve months, grey bar plan versus colored bar actual. Your exercise is different from theirs. They learn to OPERATE the levers: RACI, sprint cadence, stakeholder steering, capacity plan, budget buffer. You learn to look at a failing board and diagnose which lever is missing from the failure stories - because in the real year, what you control is not the toggle, it is the question you ask and the fix you fund.

LiveThe exec read: endpoints, ladder, and the one amber6 min▶

Three facts anchor the board, and they are the simulator's exact canon:

  • With no levers on: 1 of 10 on time, +38% over budget. That is what "we hired great people and trusted the process" buys a first-year data office.
  • With all five levers on: 9 of 10 on time, +1% variance. The ladder in between (1 → 2 → 4 → 6 → 9 as RACI, sprints, steering, capacity, buffer come on; variance +38% → +28% → +22% → +17% → +12% → +1%) prices each discipline in months and money.
  • 9 of 10 is the ceiling, on purpose. Sales forecasting carries irreducible model risk no lever removes. With sprints + steering on, it becomes an amber "re-baselined" - the Part 2 decision, visible on the board - never a green. A portfolio promising 10 for 10 is promising you a surprise.

The leader's translation table: RACI, sprints, and steering move dates. Capacity is the single biggest schedule jump (6 → 9). The buffer moves money - it never rescues a date, it rescues the year-end number. So when a board is bleeding, your first question is which currency it is bleeding in.

Honesty rails. This is a teaching simulation: the failure patterns are scripted from real portfolio pathologies, while the arithmetic on top is computed live in your browser. It is not a benchmark and its numbers are its own labeled canon - the lesson is the shape of the responses, which is very real. And the goal was never 10 of 10; it is that every slip is seen early enough to be a decision.
LiveThe red button, from the sponsor's chair4 min▶

"Crash it: add headcount to late projects" is the most natural sentence a sponsor ever says - it feels generous, decisive, and leaderly. Press it at zero levers and read the canon result: on-time delivery does not improve at all, every late project gets about a month later, and the overrun jumps from +38% to +61%. More money, more people, less portfolio.

The mechanism is Brooks's law (The Mythical Man-Month, 1975): "adding manpower to a late software project makes it later." New people must be ramped up by exactly the people who have no time, and communication paths grow as n(n-1)/2 - a team of 4 manages 6 paths, a team of 8 manages 28. The sponsor's alternative purchases that DO work: scope relief on the late project, a contractor on the upstream blocker (isolated work, cheap ramp-up), or funding the next project's start on time.

Real world

The question that replaces the button. A sponsor who has internalized this board stops saying "what do you need - more people?" and starts saying "which lever is this project missing, and what does that lever cost?" At Northgate's month-6 steering, that one substitution turns a $200k emergency-hiring conversation into a $0 capacity re-allocation (the a4 matrix had a double-booked engineer all along). The best portfolio intervention of the year was a question.

Self-studyBoard-reading drills for the next real steering meeting3 min read▶

Three habits to carry from the simulated board to your real one:

  • Read the notes before the colors. Every red row on this board carries a failure story keyed to its missing lever. Real portfolios are the same: the RAG color tells you almost nothing; the one-line cause tells you which discipline to fund.
  • Cluster before reacting. One late project is a project problem. Three late projects sharing a cause - unowned sources, double-booked specialists, unmetered spend - is a portfolio problem, and lever-shaped.
  • Ask for the lever, not the miracle. "Get it back on track" is not an ask. "Stand up the capacity plan and show me the allocation matrix next steering" is. Executives get what they specify.

Full mechanics, the rung-by-rung ladder walk, and the re-baseline scripting live in b5 - worth reading even from the leader track, if only to know what your practitioners are being taught to say to you.

Build-along

Pull the levers, then press the button ★ 18 min · everyone drives

Pairs: one drives, one plays the sponsor who only sees the board monthly. Swap halfway. The exit skill is diagnosis at a glance - failure story in, missing lever out.

Baseline read. All levers off: 1 of 10, +38%. The sponsor reads three red rows aloud; the driver names the missing lever for each BEFORE toggling anything. Disagreements are the good part.

Climb the ladder in the recommended order - RACI, sprints, steering, capacity, buffer - and watch 1 → 2 → 4 → 6 → 9 while variance falls +38% → +1%. At each rung the sponsor asks: which projects moved, and would I have funded this lever last month?

Press CRASH at zero levers. On-time stays 1 of 10, every late project slips another month, variance jumps to +61%. The sponsor says the n(n-1)/2 reason out loud - in their own words, because they are the one who will be tempted in real life.

Find the amber. All levers on: 9 of 10, and sales forecasting reads amber "re-baselined." Connect it to Part 2 - rehearse the leader's receiving line: "thank you for telling us in March."

Translate to your portfolio. Each person names their real portfolio's weakest lever, the project it is currently costing, and the specific ask they will make at their next steering. Write the ask down - that sentence is the course's takeaway.

Before you go lead

Homework ◐ 30 min

  • Draft your portfolio's one-page health scorecard: the four dials plus a leading row, and mark each indicator leading or lagging. If everything is lagging, that is the finding.
  • Pick one in-flight project and run the gate question honestly: what does the next dollar buy - kill, pivot, or re-baseline? Write three priced options as if for steering.
  • Write the lever ask: your portfolio's weakest lever, the project it is costing, and the one-sentence request you will make at the next steering meeting. Then make it.
Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Every project reports green, but validation pass rates have slipped three weeks running and pipeline run times are climbing. What is the leader's read?

Leading indicators (velocity, validation, run times) predict; lagging indicators (on-time %, variance) report. When they disagree, the leading row wins - that is the entire reason the scorecard carries it. Re-confirming green in writing just collects politer optimism.

2 · At a gate, the forecasting model hits the accuracy bar for two SKU families but cannot support fresh this year. A director argues "we've spent too much to ship half a forecast." What does the gate discipline say?

Sunk cost is not a reason - the gate prices only the future. The value is real and the problem is right; the plan's assumption (fresh data quality) was wrong. That is the re-baseline exit by definition: same goal, new slice, said early enough to be a decision instead of a scandal.

3 · Month 6, three projects late, and a sponsor proposes adding headcount to all three. What does the board show if they get their way?

The crash button is the canon's cautionary tale: at zero levers, +38% becomes +61% with zero schedule gain. Ramp-up steals the busiest people's time and communication paths grow as n(n-1)/2. The purchases that work: scope relief, upstream contractors, or the next project's on-time start.
